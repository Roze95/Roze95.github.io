Lecture 1

Matrices, Linearity, and Finite Differences

Wednesday, September 9, 2026 MW 9:30–11:00, 6-120 Reading: Strang §1.1–1.2

After this lecture you should be able to
  • Explain why matrix multiplication is defined the way it is, and read a matrix–vector product both by rows and by columns.
  • State the superposition property and recognise differentiation as a linear operator.
  • Derive forward, backward, and centred difference formulas and give their order of accuracy from Taylor series.
  • Assemble the second-difference operator as a product of two first-difference matrices.

What this course is about

18.085 is about one move, performed over and over: take a continuous problem you cannot solve — a differential equation, an equilibrium, a signal — replace it by a finite problem a computer can solve, and then understand what was gained and what was lost.

Almost every continuous problem in this class is linear, and almost every finite replacement is a matrix. So the course really has two halves that keep meeting: the linear algebra of structured matrices, and the physics and analysis of the problems those matrices came from. Today we start at the join.

1 · Matrices are compact notation for linear functions

A matrix is not primarily a grid of numbers; it is a bookkeeping device for a linear function. Take two independent variables $x_1, x_2$ and the pair of outputs

\[ y_1 = 1\,x_1 + 2\,x_2, \qquad y_2 = -3\,x_1 + 4\,x_2 , \]

which we write as

\[ \begin{bmatrix} y_1 \\ y_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ -3 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Read that product by rows and you get the two scalar equations back: row $i$ dotted with $\vec x$ gives $y_i$. Read it by columns and you get something more useful:

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ -3 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix} x_1 + \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} x_2 . \]
Key idea

$\mat A \vec x$ is a linear combination of the columns of $\mat A$, with the entries of $\vec x$ as coefficients. The set of all such outputs is the column space. Almost every solvability question in this course is really the question “is $\vec b$ in the column space of $\mat A$?”

Matrix–matrix multiplication is composition

Now feed $\vec y$ into a second linear function, this time landing in $\R^3$:

\[ z_1 = 0\,y_1 - 1\,y_2, \quad z_2 = 3\,y_1 + 0\,y_2, \quad z_3 = -2\,y_1 + 1\,y_2, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \vec z = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 3 & 0 \\ -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \vec y . \]

Can we get $\vec z$ directly from $\vec x$? Substitute $y_1 = x_1 + 2x_2$ and $y_2 = -3x_1 + 4x_2$:

\[ z_1 = 0(x_1 + 2x_2) - 1(-3x_1 + 4x_2) = \bigl(0\cdot 1 + (-1)(-3)\bigr) x_1 + \bigl(0\cdot 2 + (-1)\cdot 4\bigr) x_2 = 3x_1 - 4x_2 , \]

and similarly $z_2 = 3x_1 + 6x_2$, $z_3 = -5x_1 + 0\,x_2$. In matrix form

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 3 & 0 \\ -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ -3 & 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & -4 \\ 3 & 6 \\ -5 & 0 \end{bmatrix}. \]
Key idea

The rule “row of the first times column of the second” is not a convention someone chose. It is exactly what falls out of substituting one linear function into another. Matrix multiplication is composition of linear maps — which also explains why the inner dimensions must agree, and why $\mat A\mat B \neq \mat B \mat A$ in general (doing two things in the other order is a different thing).

Solving is running the function backwards

If a linear function takes an input to an output, then solving

\[ \mat A \vec x = \vec b \]

asks: given the output, what was the input? When that question always has exactly one answer, $\mat A$ is invertible. Much of the first month is about when that happens, what to do when it does not, and how to answer it at a cost that scales acceptably.

2 · Superposition: why linearity is worth so much

A function $f$ is linear precisely when

\[ f(a\vec x + b\vec y) = a\,f(\vec x) + b\,f(\vec y) \]

for all scalars $a,b$ and all inputs $\vec x, \vec y$.

Key idea

Superposition is why linear algebra sits at the heart of applied mathematics: it lets us break a complicated problem into simple pieces, solve the pieces, and add. We will use this again and again in different costumes — eigenvector expansions, Fourier series, Green's functions, finite element bases — and each time it is this one identity.

3 · Differentiation is a linear operator

Differentiation is a linear function whose inputs are themselves functions (a functional, or better, an operator):

\[ D[x^2] = 2x, \qquad D[e^x] = e^x, \qquad D[\,4x^2 - 5e^x\,] = 8x - 5e^x = 4\,D[x^2] - 5\,D[e^x]. \]

So we can study differentiation — and therefore differential equations — with the tools of linear algebra.

There is one obstruction. The space of functions is infinite-dimensional, while a computer has finite storage and we want answers in finite time.

Key idea · Discretisation

Replace the function $u(x)$ by its values on a finite grid, and the derivative operator by a matrix acting on that vector of values. Everything else in this course is a consequence of choosing that replacement well.

4 · Finite differences

Start from the definition

\[ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}, \]

and simply stop short of the limit: keep $h$ finite. Three natural choices:

\[ \underbrace{f'(x) \approx \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}}_{\text{forward}}, \qquad \underbrace{f'(x) \approx \frac{f(x) - f(x-h)}{h}}_{\text{backward}}, \qquad \underbrace{f'(x) \approx \frac{f(x+h) - f(x-h)}{2h}}_{\text{centred}}. \]

Error analysis is just Taylor series

Expand

\[ f(x+h) = f(x) + h f'(x) + \frac{h^2}{2} f''(x) + \frac{h^3}{6}f'''(x) + \cdots \]

and divide:

\[ \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} = f'(x) + \underbrace{\frac{h}{2} f''(x) + \cdots}_{\text{error}} = f'(x) + \Oh{h}. \]

So forward (and backward) differences are first-order accurate. For the centred difference the even-order terms survive and the odd ones cancel:

\[ \frac{f(x+h) - f(x-h)}{2h} = f'(x) + \frac{h^2}{6} f'''(x) + \cdots = f'(x) + \Oh{h^2}. \]
Remark · The bargain of this course

One extra function evaluation bought us a whole order of accuracy. Halving $h$ cuts the forward-difference error in half but the centred error by a factor of four. Nearly every scheme we meet is a trade of this kind — accuracy against cost, stencil width, or stability — and the Taylor expansion is how we price it.

Second derivatives

Apply first differences twice: a backward difference of a forward difference,

\[ f''(x) \approx \frac{f'(x) - f'(x-h)}{h} \approx \frac{\dfrac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} - \dfrac{f(x)-f(x-h)}{h}}{h} = \boxed{\ \frac{f(x+h) - 2f(x) + f(x-h)}{h^2}\ } \]

which is the three-point centred scheme, and it is $\Oh{h^2}$ accurate. This $1, -2, 1$ stencil is the single most important object in 18.085; we will meet it as a matrix within the hour and keep meeting it until the last lecture.

5 · The “infinite matrix” view

If a function is represented by its values on a grid,

\[ f \;\longleftrightarrow\; \begin{bmatrix} \vdots \\ f(x_0-h) \\ f(x_0) \\ f(x_0+h) \\ \vdots \end{bmatrix}, \]

then a difference formula is a linear operation on that vector, i.e. a matrix. Backward differences, $\frac{f(x)-f(x-h)}{h} = \frac1h f(x) - \frac1h f(x-h)$, are

\[ \mat A = \frac{1}{h} \begin{bmatrix} \ddots & \ddots & & \\ & -1 & 1 & \\ & & -1 & 1 \\ & & & \ddots & \ddots \end{bmatrix}, \]

and forward differences, $\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$, give the same band shifted by one:

\[ \mat B = \frac{1}{h} \begin{bmatrix} \ddots & \ddots & & \\ -1 & 1 & & \\ & -1 & 1 & \\ & & \ddots & \ddots \end{bmatrix}. \]

Multiplying them composes the two operations, and out falls the second difference:

\[ \mat A \mat B = \frac{1}{h^2} \begin{bmatrix} \ddots & \ddots & \ddots & \\ 1 & -2 & 1 & \\ & 1 & -2 & 1 \\ & & \ddots & \ddots & \ddots \end{bmatrix}. \]
Key idea

The second-difference matrix factors. Writing it as $\mat A\mat B$ — and soon, on a bounded domain with boundary conditions, as $\mat A\T \mat C \mat A$ — is not a curiosity. That factored form is where symmetry, positive definiteness, energy, and the whole framework of equilibrium problems come from.

Watch out · The boundary is where the content is

These matrices are infinite, which is a fiction. On a real interval the grid stops, and what you assume at the two ends changes the matrix, its invertibility, and the physics it models. That is the next lecture, and it is not a technicality.

Where this is going

Next time: truncate the grid, impose fixed–fixed, fixed–free, and free–free boundary conditions, and meet Strang's matrices $\Kmat, \Tmat, \Bmat, \Cmat$. The difference between an invertible $\Kmat$ and a singular $\Bmat$ will turn out to be a statement about physics, not about bookkeeping.

Companion code not part of the notes

Ten lines that reproduce the order-of-accuracy claim above. Run it, halve $h$, and watch the two error columns fall by 2 and by 4.

import numpy as np
f, df = np.sin, np.cos
x = 1.0
for h in [1e-1, 5e-2, 2.5e-2, 1.25e-2]:
    fwd = (f(x+h) - f(x)) / h
    ctr = (f(x+h) - f(x-h)) / (2*h)
    print(f"{h:8.5f}  {abs(fwd-df(x)):.3e}  {abs(ctr-df(x)):.3e}")

The same in MATLAB:

x = 1; 
for h = [1e-1 5e-2 2.5e-2 1.25e-2]
    fwd = (sin(x+h) - sin(x)) / h;
    ctr = (sin(x+h) - sin(x-h)) / (2*h);
    fprintf('%8.5f  %.3e  %.3e\n', h, abs(fwd-cos(x)), abs(ctr-cos(x)));
end
