Lecture 4

Elimination and the LU Decomposition

Monday, September 21, 2026 MW 9:30–11:00, 6-120 Reading: Strang §1.3

Download PDF

After this lecture you should be able to
  • Carry out Gaussian elimination on a small system and finish it by back substitution.
  • Write a single row operation as multiplication by a lower triangular matrix, and read off $\mat A = \mat L\mat U$.
  • Eliminate on $\Tmat$ and on $\Kmat$ and write down the resulting $\mat L$ and $\mat U$.
  • Explain why a symmetric matrix comes out of elimination as $\mat L\mat D\mat L\T$.
  • Read elimination on a resistor ladder as node elimination, a pivot as the conductance that is left, and one $\mat L\mat U$ as the factorisation reused at every step of a transient.

From building matrices to solving with them

Last lecture produced four matrices. None of them is any use until we can answer $\mat A\vec x = \vec b$. Today's answer is the one every piece of numerical software actually uses, and the point of the lecture is that it is not really an algorithm — it is a factorisation. Once you see elimination as $\mat A = \mat L\mat U$, the arithmetic you did by hand becomes a reusable object.

1 · Gaussian elimination: two views of one calculation

The standard technique for a general linear system is Gaussian elimination. Here it is twice: once as the variable elimination you already know, once as row reduction of a matrix.

Variable elimination

\[ \begin{aligned} x + 3y &= -3 \\ -2x + y &= -8 \end{aligned} \]

$\Big\downarrow$

cancel out the $x$ term in the second equation

\[ \begin{aligned} x + 3y &= -3 \\ 7y &= -14 \end{aligned} \]

Matrix row reduction

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ -2 & 1\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ -8 \end{bmatrix} \]

$\Big\downarrow$

make the matrix upper triangular

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 7\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ -14 \end{bmatrix} \]

Either way, the last equation now involves one unknown, so we solve upwards — back substitution:

\[ 7y = -14 \ \Longrightarrow\ y = -2, \qquad x + 3(-2) = -3 \ \Longrightarrow\ x = 3 . \]
Key idea · The general idea

Given a system $\mat A\vec x = \vec b$, do row operations on $\mat A$ (and on $\vec b$) to obtain an upper triangular system, which can then be solved by back substitution. The two columns above are the same calculation; the right-hand one just stops writing the letters $x$ and $y$ down.

2 · The LU decomposition: elimination as matrix multiplication

The LU decomposition is the matrix representation of Gaussian elimination. To get there, notice that the row operation we just performed is itself a matrix multiplication. Start from

\[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ -2 & 1\end{bmatrix}}_{\mat A} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ -8 \end{bmatrix}}_{\vec b} \]

and multiply both sides on the left by the matrix that adds twice the first row to the second:

\[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1\end{bmatrix}}_{\mat L\inv} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ -2 & 1\end{bmatrix}}_{\mat A} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1\end{bmatrix}}_{\mat L\inv} \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ -8 \end{bmatrix} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 7\end{bmatrix}}_{\mat U} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ -14 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mat L\inv \vec b} . \]

So the whole elimination is the single statement

\[ \mat L\inv \mat A = \mat U \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \boxed{\ \mat A = \mat L\mat U\ } \]

Note that the inverse of a lower triangular matrix is still lower triangular, so $\mat L$ is lower triangular too. Here inverting the step just flips the sign of the multiplier, giving $\mat L = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -2 & 1\end{smallmatrix}\right]$, and indeed

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -2 & 1\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 7\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ -2 & -6+7\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ -2 & 1\end{bmatrix} = \mat A . \]
Key idea · What the factorisation buys you

$\mat L$ is a record of the elimination steps and $\mat U$ is the triangular system they produced. Solving $\mat A\vec x = \vec b$ becomes two triangular solves: $\mat L\vec c = \vec b$ forwards, then $\mat U\vec x = \vec c$ backwards. The expensive part — the elimination — depended only on $\mat A$, so a second right-hand side is nearly free.

More than one step

Larger matrices need several row operations. Applying them one after another to $\mat A\vec x = \vec b$ gives

\[ \underbrace{\bigl(\mat L_n\inv \cdots \mat L_1\inv\bigr)}_{\text{gives an upper triangular }\mat U} \mat A\,\vec x = \bigl(\mat L_n\inv \cdots \mat L_1\inv\bigr)\vec b , \]

so that $\mat L_n\inv \cdots \mat L_1\inv \mat A = \mat U$, and therefore

\[ \mat A = \bigl(\mat L_1 \cdots \mat L_n\bigr)\mat U = \mat L\mat U . \]

The individual matrices $\mat L_j$ and $\mat L_j\inv$ for any single row operation are simple to write down and to invert. For example,

\[ \mat L_j\inv = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & & & \\ 0 & 1 & & \\ \vdots & \ell_j & \ddots & \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mat L_j = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & & & \\ 0 & 1 & & \\ \vdots & -\ell_j & \ddots & \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

— just a change in sign of the factor.

Watch out · Do not assemble $\mat L$ by guessing

The inverse of a product of several $\mat L_j$ is not necessarily as simple as putting the various $-\ell_j$ together in their various positions. It depends on the order in which the steps were taken. Work a $3\times 3$ case out by hand before you trust the pattern — this is on the problem set.

3 · Worked example: free–fixed centred differences, $\Tmat$

Take the free–fixed second-difference matrix from last lecture, here at size $4$:

\[ \Tmat = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 & & \\ -1 & 2 & -1 & \\ & -1 & 2 & -1 \\ & & -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \ \longrightarrow\ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 & & \\ 0 & 1 & -1 & \\ & -1 & 2 & -1 \\ & & -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \quad \ell_{21} = 1 \] \[ \longrightarrow\ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 & & \\ 0 & 1 & -1 & \\ & 0 & 1 & -1 \\ & & -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \quad \ell_{32} = 1 \qquad \longrightarrow\ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 & & \\ 0 & 1 & -1 & \\ & 0 & 1 & -1 \\ & & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \quad \ell_{43} = 1 . \]

Every pivot came out equal to $1$ and every multiplier equal to $1$, so

\[ \mat L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & & & \\ -1 & 1 & & \\ & -1 & 1 & \\ & & -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mat U = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 & & \\ & 1 & -1 & \\ & & 1 & -1 \\ & & & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \Tmat = \mat L\mat U . \]

Look at those two factors. $\mat U$ is exactly $\mat L\T$, and both are bidiagonal: a single first difference. The second-difference matrix has come apart into two first-difference matrices — which is precisely how we built it in Lecture 2, now recovered by elimination rather than by construction.

4 · Worked example: fixed–fixed centred differences, $\Kmat$

Now the fixed–fixed matrix, where the corner entries are $2$ instead of $1$ and the arithmetic is less tidy:

\[ \Kmat = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & & \\ -1 & 2 & -1 & \\ & -1 & 2 & -1 \\ & & -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \ \longrightarrow\ \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & & \\ 0 & \tfrac{3}{2} & -1 & \\ & -1 & 2 & -1 \\ & & -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \quad \ell_{21} = \tfrac{1}{2} \] \[ \longrightarrow\ \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & & \\ 0 & \tfrac{3}{2} & -1 & \\ & 0 & \tfrac{4}{3} & -1 \\ & & -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \quad \ell_{32} = \tfrac{2}{3} \qquad \longrightarrow\ \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & & \\ 0 & \tfrac{3}{2} & -1 & \\ & 0 & \tfrac{4}{3} & -1 \\ & & 0 & \tfrac{5}{4} \end{bmatrix} \quad \ell_{43} = \tfrac{3}{4} , \]

the last of which is $\mat U$. Collecting the multipliers with their signs flipped,

\[ \mat L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & & & \\ -\tfrac{1}{2} & 1 & & \\ & -\tfrac{2}{3} & 1 & \\ & & -\tfrac{3}{4} & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mat U = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & & \\ & \tfrac{3}{2} & -1 & \\ & & \tfrac{4}{3} & -1 \\ & & & \tfrac{5}{4} \end{bmatrix}. \]
Remark · The pivots are not random

The pivots came out $2,\ \tfrac{3}{2},\ \tfrac{4}{3},\ \tfrac{5}{4}$ — the pattern $\tfrac{k+1}{k}$. What would the next one be if we had eliminated on the $5\times5$ version? Structured matrices give structured factorisations, and that is exactly why it is worth knowing that a matrix is $\Kmat$ rather than just “some tridiagonal matrix”.

5 · Symmetry survives: $\Kmat = \mat L\mat D\mat L\T$

The $\mat L$ and $\mat U$ above do not look like transposes of each other, but they nearly are: $\mat U$ is $\mat L\T$ with the rows rescaled. So extract a factor from each row of $\mat U$ to make the diagonal entries equal to $1$:

\[ \mat U = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & & & \\ & \tfrac{3}{2} & & \\ & & \tfrac{4}{3} & \\ & & & \tfrac{5}{4} \end{bmatrix}}_{\mat D} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -\tfrac{1}{2} & & \\ & 1 & -\tfrac{2}{3} & \\ & & 1 & -\tfrac{3}{4} \\ & & & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{this is } \mat L\T} . \]

Therefore

\[ \boxed{\ \Kmat = \mat L\mat D\mat L\T\ } \]

The $\mat L\mat D\mat L\T$ form is not surprising, given that we started with a symmetric matrix $\Kmat$. And $\mat L\mat D\mat L\T$ is manifestly symmetric — you do not have to compute anything to see it:

\[ \bigl(\mat L\mat D\mat L\T\bigr)\T = \bigl(\mat L\T\bigr)\T \mat D\T \mat L\T = \mat L\mat D\mat L\T , \]

using only that $\mat D$ is diagonal, hence its own transpose.

Key idea · Symmetry in, symmetry out

Elimination on a symmetric matrix does not destroy its symmetry; it relocates it. All of the asymmetry of $\mat L\mat U$ was bookkeeping — the scaling of the rows — and pulling that scaling out into $\mat D$ leaves the same triangular factor on both sides. The information that used to be spread over the whole matrix now sits in one triangle and one diagonal.

Remark · Watch the diagonal

All four pivots of $\Kmat$ came out positive. That is not an accident either, and it is not just aesthetic: when we get to positive definite matrices later in this arc, the signs of the entries of $\mat D$ will be the test we run.

6 · Physical example: $\Kmat$ and $\Tmat$ as a resistor ladder

The same two matrices come out of a circuit, and the circuit explains the pivots. Put four nodes in a line, join each neighbouring pair by a $1\,\Omega$ resistor, ground both ends, and inject a current $b_k$ into node $k$. The unknowns are the node voltages $v_k$.

Kirchhoff's current law at node $k$ says that the current injected there leaves through the two resistors touching it, and Ohm's law turns each of those currents into a voltage difference:

\[ \frac{v_k - v_{k-1}}{1\,\Omega} + \frac{v_k - v_{k+1}}{1\,\Omega} = b_k \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad -v_{k-1} + 2 v_k - v_{k+1} = b_k . \]

That is the interior row of $\Kmat$, with $v_0 = v_5 = 0$ at the grounded ends. Cut the resistor at the left end instead and node $1$ has only one resistor attached, so its row loses a term and reads $v_1 - v_2 = b_1$ — the $1$ in the corner of $\Tmat$. This is Lecture 3's key idea in electrical dress: a grounded node is a held end, an open node is a free end, and the whole difference is one number in one corner.

Key idea · A pivot is the conductance you have left

Eliminating node $1$ is deleting node $1$ from the circuit: its equation is used to write $v_1$ in terms of $v_2$, and what remains is the network the other nodes still see. So each pivot is the total conductance at the node being eliminated, looking into whatever is still attached to it.

Walk that down the ladder of $\Kmat$. Node $1$ sees $1\,$S to ground and $1\,$S to node $2$: pivot $2$. With node $1$ absorbed, node $2$ looks left through two unit resistors in series, worth $\tfrac{1}{2}\,$S, and still sees $1\,$S on its right: pivot $\tfrac{3}{2}$. After $k-1$ eliminations the path to ground on the left is $k$ resistors in series, so

\[ \text{pivot}_k \;=\; \underbrace{1}_{\text{right}} + \underbrace{\frac{1}{k}}_{\text{left}} \;=\; \frac{k+1}{k} , \]

which is the pattern of the previous remark, now with a reason — and the fifth pivot must be $\tfrac{6}{5}$. For $\Tmat$ the left end is open, so the chain behind node $k$ is a dead end carrying no current and contributing no conductance; every pivot is the single unit conductance on the right, $1$, exactly as the arithmetic gave.

The triangular solves are physical too. For $\Tmat = \mat L\mat U$ with $\mat U = \mat L\T$, the forward solve $\mat L\vec c = \vec b$ reads $c_k - c_{k-1} = b_k$, so $c_k = b_1 + \cdots + b_k$: with the far end open, every current injected to the left of resistor $k$ has nowhere to go but through it, and $c_k$ is that current. The back solve $\mat L\T\vec v = \vec c$ then reads $v_k - v_{k+1} = c_k$, which is Ohm's law across that resistor. The factors are the ladder's difference matrix $\mat A$ and its transpose, $\Tmat = \mat A\T\mat A$, and elimination has split the problem into conservation first and constitutive law second — the split that will organise the springs-and-masses lecture and everything after it.

7 · Physical example: an RC ladder, one factorisation for every time step

Hang a capacitor $C$ from each node of the same ladder down to ground, keep both ends of the ladder grounded, and let the injected currents vary in time. Kirchhoff's law at node $k$ now carries a charging term, and in vector form

\[ C\,\dd{\vec v}{t} + \frac{1}{R}\,\Kmat\vec v = \vec b(t) . \]

Step it forward implicitly — evaluate the resistive term at the new time, which is backward Euler, properly in the time-stepping lecture:

\[ C\,\frac{\vec v^{\,n+1} - \vec v^{\,n}}{\Delta t} + \frac{1}{R}\,\Kmat\vec v^{\,n+1} = \vec b^{\,n+1} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\ \bigl(\eye + \alpha\Kmat\bigr)\vec v^{\,n+1} = \vec v^{\,n} + \frac{\Delta t}{C}\,\vec b^{\,n+1}\ }, \quad \alpha = \frac{\Delta t}{RC} . \]

Every time step is a solve with the same $\mat M = \eye + \alpha\Kmat$: tridiagonal, symmetric, and with a heavier diagonal than $\Kmat$ itself. Taking $\alpha = 1$, a step of one time constant $RC$,

\[ \mat M = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & -1 & & \\ -1 & 3 & -1 & \\ & -1 & 3 & -1 \\ & & -1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mat M = \mat L\mat D\mat L\T, \quad \mat D = \operatorname{diag}\!\left(3,\ \tfrac{8}{3},\ \tfrac{21}{8},\ \tfrac{55}{21}\right), \]

with multipliers $\ell_{21} = \tfrac{1}{3}$, $\ell_{32} = \tfrac{3}{8}$, $\ell_{43} = \tfrac{8}{21}$ in the same convention as above. Symmetric matrix in, $\mat L\mat D\mat L\T$ out, all four pivots positive — one triangle and one diagonal is the whole factorisation.

Now the point. $\mat M$ is built from the circuit and the step size; the right-hand side $\vec v^{\,n} + (\Delta t/C)\,\vec b^{\,n+1}$ is built from the present state and the input signal. Only the second changes as the simulation runs. So factor $\mat M$ once, before the loop, and spend each of the thousand time steps on two triangular solves — which is the “a second right-hand side is nearly free” idea from the beginning of the lecture, now paying for itself a thousand times over.

Watch out · Never form $\mat M\inv$, and watch $\Delta t$

$\mat M\inv$ is a full matrix, while $\mat L$ and $\mat U$ keep the two nonzero diagonals of $\mat M$: forming the inverse throws away the sparsity you started with. And $\alpha$ sits inside $\mat M$, so changing $\Delta t$ means a new matrix and a new factorisation — which is exactly why implicit solvers hold the step fixed for long stretches. Here the factorisation and the solve are both $\Oh{n}$ and the saving is a constant factor; on the two-dimensional grids later in the term the factorisation costs far more than the solve, and re-using it is the difference between a simulation that runs and one that does not.

Where this is going

Elimination answers “solve $\mat A\vec x = \vec b$” mechanically, without ever asking what the columns of $\mat A$ are doing. Next time we ask: reading $\mat A\vec x = \vec b$ as a request to build $\vec b$ out of the columns of $\mat A$, when is that possible, when is it unique, and what changes if we choose a different set of building blocks?

Companion code not part of the notes

The RC ladder of §7, run for a thousand steps on one factorisation. The factorisation happens on the line before the loop; each step inside it is two triangular solves.

import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import lu_factor, lu_solve

n = 4
K = np.diag(2*np.ones(n)) - np.diag(np.ones(n-1), 1) - np.diag(np.ones(n-1), -1)
M = np.eye(n) + K                      # alpha = 1: one step per time constant RC
lu, piv = lu_factor(M)                 # factor ONCE, outside the loop

v, b = np.zeros(n), np.ones(n)         # start at rest, constant injected current
for step in range(1000):
    v = lu_solve((lu, piv), v + b)     # two triangular solves, nothing more
print(v)                               # settles on the steady state K v = b

Two things worth checking by hand. Replace the loop body with np.linalg.solve(M, v + b) and you re-factor a thousand times for the same answer. And np.linalg.inv(M) is dense in all sixteen entries, while $\mat L$ and $\mat U$ between them hold seven — the sparsity argument of the warning above, at $n=4$ where you can still see it.
