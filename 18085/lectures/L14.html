Lecture 15

Time Stepping: Euler, Leapfrog, Trapezoidal

Monday, November 2, 2026 MW 9:30–11:00, 6-120 Reading: Strang §2.2, §6.2

Download PDF

After this lecture you should be able to
  • Derive forward Euler, backward Euler, leapfrog and the trapezoidal method from Taylor series or from a rule for the integral of $\vec f$ over one step.
  • Write each method as a growth matrix $\mat G$ for the oscillator $\ddot u + u = 0$.
  • Decide stability from the eigenvalues of $\mat G$, and read off what each method does to the energy and to the phase.
  • Find the step limit $h < 2$ of leapfrog and the quantity it conserves.
  • Draw each method as an area under the curve of $\vec f$, and read off what one step misses.

1 · Forward Euler, from Lecture 14

We begin with the derivation of forward Euler from last time, as it was given there.

The mass–spring system is also a good toy problem for touching upon finite difference methods for time-based differential equations, also known as dynamical systems.

For simplicity, let us shrink all the way down to one mass and one spring. The system is

\[ \ddot u + u = 0, \]

which is a second-order system.

First insight: lower the order by adding variables

We can lower the order of the system by introducing new variables. Define

\[ v \equiv \dot u, \qquad \text{i.e.} \qquad \frac{du}{dt} = v, \]

and then $\ddot u + u = 0$ says exactly

\[ \frac{dv}{dt} = -u . \]

Taken together we now have only first-order time derivatives:

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \end{bmatrix}. \]

Interpretation

$v$ is the velocity of the mass on the spring. Instead of a single second-order equation in the displacement $u$ alone, we are studying an equivalent system of first-order equations in $u$ and $v$.

In general, a system of first-order differential equations with respect to time is called a dynamical system. In principle any kind of higher-order time dynamics can be reduced to a dynamical system with only first derivatives in time, through the introduction of new variables such as $v$.

One canonical form

Order is not a fundamental property of a time-dependent problem: it is a choice of variables. Reduce everything to $\dot{\vec y} = \mat A\vec y$ and you only ever need one family of time-stepping methods, applied to a vector.

Phase space

To visualise the time evolution of dynamical systems we often plot the trajectories of the dependent variables in a phase space diagram. “Phase space” is simply a technical term for the possible values the dependent variables can take on.

For the mass–spring system, $\dot u = v$ and $\dot v = -u$. Note that

\[ \begin{bmatrix} \dot u & \dot v \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \end{bmatrix} = \dot u\,u + \dot v\,v = (v)\,u + (-u)\,v = uv - uv = 0 . \]

So the time derivative of the vector $\begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \end{bmatrix}$ is always orthogonal to it. A vector whose rate of change is perpendicular to itself never changes length. Therefore the phase-space trajectory $\begin{bmatrix} u(t) \\ v(t) \end{bmatrix}$ is a circle.

The circle is conservation of energy

The radius $\sqrt{u^2 + v^2}$ is fixed by the energy in the system, which the true dynamics conserve. Everything interesting about the numerical methods below is a statement about what they do to that radius.

Discretising time

Suppose we now discretise time. Put

\[ t_n = n\,\Delta t, \qquad U_n \equiv u(t_n), \qquad V_n \equiv v(t_n), \]

and replace each time derivative by a forward difference,

\[ \left.\frac{du}{dt}\right|_{t_n} \simeq \frac{U_{n+1} - U_n}{\Delta t}, \qquad \left.\frac{dv}{dt}\right|_{t_n} \simeq \frac{V_{n+1} - V_n}{\Delta t}. \]

So the two equations of the dynamical system become

\[ \frac{U_{n+1} - U_n}{\Delta t} = v, \qquad \frac{V_{n+1} - V_n}{\Delta t} = -u . \]
The whole question in one line

What grid points should we use on the right-hand side? The left-hand sides are settled; the right-hand sides still say $u$ and $v$ with no subscript. Different choices of which time level to evaluate them at lead to different methods, and to completely different long-time behaviour.

Forward Euler

Take everything on the right at the old time level:

\[ \text{RHS} = \begin{bmatrix} V_n \\ -U_n \end{bmatrix}. \]

We obtain

\[ \begin{cases} \dfrac{U_{n+1} - U_n}{\Delta t} = V_n \\[2ex] \dfrac{V_{n+1} - V_n}{\Delta t} = -U_n \end{cases} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \begin{cases} U_{n+1} = U_n + \Delta t\,V_n \\ V_{n+1} = V_n - \Delta t\,U_n \end{cases} \]

which is explicit: the new values are written down directly from the old ones. In matrix form,

\[ \begin{bmatrix} U_{n+1} \\ V_{n+1} \end{bmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & \Delta t \\ -\Delta t & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mat G_F} \begin{bmatrix} U_n \\ V_n \end{bmatrix}, \]

and $\mat G_F$ is called the growth matrix. The end result of $n$ steps is

\[ \begin{bmatrix} U_n \\ V_n \end{bmatrix} = \mat G_F^{\,n}\begin{bmatrix} U_0 \\ V_0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \text{with} \quad \begin{bmatrix} U_0 \\ V_0 \end{bmatrix} \ \text{the initial conditions.} \]

Qualitatively, what happens to $\begin{bmatrix} U_n \\ V_n \end{bmatrix}$ as $n$ gets large depends on the eigenvalues of $\mat G_F$. Writing $h = \Delta t$ for brevity, $\mat G_F$ has eigenvalues

\[ \lambda = 1 + ih, \qquad \lambda = 1 - ih, \qquad \text{so} \qquad \abs{\lambda} = \sqrt{1 + h^2} > 1, \]

and we will see a growth in the size of the vector $\begin{bmatrix} U_n \\ V_n \end{bmatrix}$.

What we compare

Write $\vec Y_n = (U_n, V_n)$ for the computed vector at $t_n = nh$, which approximates $\vec y(t_n) = (u(t_n), v(t_n))$, and $\mat A = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$, so that the oscillator is $\dot{\vec y} = \mat A\vec y$. Forward Euler is $\vec Y_{n+1} = \mat G_F\vec Y_n$ with $\mat G_F = \eye + h\mat A$.

Over one step of length $h$ the exact solution of the oscillator is a rotation,

\[ \vec y(t+h) = \begin{bmatrix} \cos h & \sin h \\ -\sin h & \cos h \end{bmatrix}\vec y(t), \]

which turns the point clockwise through the angle $h$ and keeps its length; its eigenvalues are $e^{\pm ih}$. Every method in this lecture replaces the rotation by a growth matrix $\mat G$, with $\vec Y_{n+1} = \mat G\vec Y_n$ and $\vec Y_n = \mat G^n\vec Y_0$.

Two questions for every method

Write the eigenvalues of $\mat G$ as $\lambda = \abs{\lambda}\,e^{\pm i\theta}$.

  • Amplitude. Is $\abs{\lambda} = 1$? If $\abs{\lambda} > 1$ the computed oscillation grows; if $\abs{\lambda} < 1$ it dies away.
  • Phase. Is $\theta = h$? If $\theta < h$ the computed oscillation falls behind the true one; if $\theta > h$ it runs ahead.

The true rotation has $\abs{\lambda} = 1$ and $\theta = h$.

The methods differ in where, inside the step from $t_n$ to $t_{n+1}$, they evaluate the right-hand side. Leapfrog staggers its two variables and gets its own picture in Section 3; the other three are here.

A closer look at forward Euler

How accurate is the forward difference?

Taylor's theorem gives

\[ u(t_{n+1}) = u(t_n) + h\,\dot u(t_n) + \frac{h^2}{2}\,\ddot u(t_n) + \cdots, \qquad \text{so} \qquad \frac{u(t_{n+1}) - u(t_n)}{h} = \dot u(t_n) + \frac h2\,\ddot u(t_n) + \cdots . \]

The forward difference approximates the derivative at the old time $t_n$ with an error of order $h$. Each step misses the term $\frac{h^2}{2}\ddot{\vec y}$; over a fixed time $T$ there are $T/h$ steps, so these errors add up to order $h$: forward Euler is a first-order method. For any system $\dot{\vec y} = \vec f(t, \vec y)$ the method reads $\vec Y_{n+1} = \vec Y_n + h\,\vec f(t_n, \vec Y_n)$. The same formula comes from the integral form

\[ \vec y(t_{n+1}) = \vec y(t_n) + \int_{t_n}^{t_{n+1}} \vec f(t, \vec y(t))\,dt, \]

if the integral is replaced by a rectangle of width $h$ whose height $\vec f(t_n, \vec Y_n)$ is taken at the left end (sketch below). The other methods of this lecture are other rules for the same integral, except leapfrog, which comes from the second-order equation itself.

Eigenvalues and energy

The eigenvalues come from

\[ \det(\mat G_F - \lambda\eye) = (1 - \lambda)^2 + h^2 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 1 - \lambda = \mp ih \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \lambda = 1 \pm ih, \]

so $\abs{\lambda} = \sqrt{1 + h^2} > 1$ and the angle per step is $\theta = \arctan h$. The energy can be followed directly. The cross terms cancel in

\[ U_{n+1}^2 + V_{n+1}^2 = (U_n + hV_n)^2 + (V_n - hU_n)^2 = (1 + h^2)(U_n^2 + V_n^2), \]

so each step multiplies the energy by $1 + h^2$, whatever the starting point (the red spiral in the figure of Section 2). Geometrically, the step $h\,(V_n, -U_n)$ is perpendicular to $(U_n, V_n)$ and $h$ times as long, so by Pythagoras the new point is $\sqrt{1 + h^2}$ times as far from the origin as the old one (left panel of the figure in Section 2). In matrix terms $\mat G_F\T\mat G_F = (1 + h^2)\,\eye$: $\mat G_F$ is $\sqrt{1 + h^2}$ times a rotation through $\arctan h = h - h^3/3 + \cdots$, an angle a little smaller than $h$. Over one period, $N$ steps of $h = 2\pi/N$, the amplitude grows by

\[ (1 + h^2)^{N/2} = \Bigl(1 + \frac{4\pi^2}{N^2}\Bigr)^{N/2} \approx e^{2\pi^2/N}. \]

The factor is 2.22 for $N = 24$ steps per period (the exponential gives 2.28) and still 1.02 for $N = 1000$.

Example 1.1 (Forward Euler by hand). Given. $\ddot u + u = 0$ with $u(0) = 1$, $v(0) = 0$, so $\vec Y_0 = (1, 0)$, and a step $h$.

Find. What are $\vec Y_1 = \mat G_F\vec Y_0$ and $\vec Y_2 = \mat G_F^2\vec Y_0$ for a general $h$? What are they for $h = 0.5$? What happens to $U_n^2 + V_n^2$, and how far is $\vec Y_2$ from the true point at $t = 1$?

Answer. Keep $h$ as a letter first:

\[ \vec Y_1 = \mat G_F\vec Y_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & h \\ -h & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -h \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mat G_F^2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & h \\ -h & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & h \\ -h & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 - h^2 & 2h \\ -2h & 1 - h^2 \end{bmatrix}, \] \[ \vec Y_2 = \mat G_F\vec Y_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 + h\,(-h) \\ -h + 1\cdot(-h) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 - h^2 \\ -2h \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \text{the same as} \qquad \vec Y_2 = \mat G_F^2\vec Y_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 - h^2 \\ -2h \end{bmatrix}. \]

The squared lengths are $\abs{\vec Y_1}^2 = 1 + h^2$ and $\abs{\vec Y_2}^2 = (1 - h^2)^2 + 4h^2 = 1 + 2h^2 + h^4 = (1 + h^2)^2$, as $\mat G_F\T\mat G_F = (1 + h^2)\,\eye$ says. Now put $h = 0.5$:

\[ \mat G_F = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0.5 \\ -0.5 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \vec Y_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -0.5 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \vec Y_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 - 0.25 \\ -2\cdot 0.5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.75 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}, \]

so $(U_1, V_1) = (1, -0.5)$ and $(U_2, V_2) = (0.75, -1)$. $U_n^2 + V_n^2$ goes $1, 1.25, 1.5625$: it is multiplied by $1 + h^2 = 1.25$ at each step. The true point is $(\cos 1, -\sin 1) = (0.540, -0.841)$, so after two steps the computed point is off by 0.263, about a quarter of the radius.

2 · Backward Euler

The same start as forward Euler

As for forward Euler, put

\[ t_n = n\,h, \qquad U_n \approx u(t_n), \qquad V_n \approx v(t_n), \qquad \vec Y_n = \begin{bmatrix} U_n \\ V_n \end{bmatrix}, \]

where $U_n$ and $V_n$ are the computed values (Lecture 14 wrote $U_n \equiv u(t_n)$), and replace each time derivative by the same difference quotient,

\[ \frac{du}{dt} \simeq \frac{U_{n+1} - U_n}{h}, \qquad \frac{dv}{dt} \simeq \frac{V_{n+1} - V_n}{h}. \]

This time read the quotient as the derivative at the new time $t_{n+1}$. Taylor's theorem about $t_{n+1}$ gives

\[ u(t_n) = u(t_{n+1}) - h\,\dot u(t_{n+1}) + \frac{h^2}{2}\,\ddot u(t_{n+1}) - \cdots, \]

so that

\[ \frac{u(t_{n+1}) - u(t_n)}{h} = \dot u(t_{n+1}) - \frac h2\,\ddot u(t_{n+1}) + \cdots, \]

the same accuracy, order $h$, as before. As in Section 1 the two equations of the dynamical system become

\[ \frac{U_{n+1} - U_n}{h} = v, \qquad \frac{V_{n+1} - V_n}{h} = -u, \]

and the question is again which time level to use on the right-hand side.

The right-hand side at the new time level

Now take everything on the right at the new time level:

\[ \text{RHS} = \begin{bmatrix} V_{n+1} \\ -U_{n+1} \end{bmatrix}. \]

We obtain

\[ \begin{cases} \dfrac{U_{n+1} - U_n}{h} = V_{n+1} \\[2ex] \dfrac{V_{n+1} - V_n}{h} = -U_{n+1} \end{cases} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \begin{cases} U_{n+1} = U_n + h\,V_{n+1} \\ V_{n+1} = V_n - h\,U_{n+1} \end{cases} \]

The unknowns now appear on both sides, so we gather them on the left:

\[ \begin{cases} U_{n+1} - h\,V_{n+1} = U_n \\ h\,U_{n+1} + V_{n+1} = V_n \end{cases} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -h \\ h & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} U_{n+1} \\ V_{n+1} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} U_n \\ V_n \end{bmatrix}, \quad \text{that is} \quad (\eye - h\mat A)\,\vec Y_{n+1} = \vec Y_n . \]

That is an implicit method: each step costs a solve. For a general system $\dot{\vec y} = \vec f(t, \vec y)$ backward Euler reads $\vec Y_{n+1} = \vec Y_n + h\,\vec f(t_{n+1}, \vec Y_{n+1})$: in the integral form, the height of the rectangle is taken at the right end (sketch below). For a nonlinear $\vec f$ each step is a nonlinear system, usually solved by Newton's method starting from $\vec Y_n$; each Newton step solves a linear system with the matrix $\eye - h\mat J$, where $\mat J = \partial\vec f/\partial\vec y$. For $\vec f = \mat A\vec y$ the Jacobian is $\mat A$ and one linear solve is enough.

Solving for the new values

Solve the $2\times 2$ system by elimination. The second equation gives $V_{n+1} = V_n - hU_{n+1}$; put it into the first:

\[ U_{n+1} - h\bigl(V_n - hU_{n+1}\bigr) = U_n \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (1 + h^2)\,U_{n+1} = U_n + hV_n \quad\Longrightarrow\quad U_{n+1} = \frac{U_n + hV_n}{1 + h^2}. \]

Then back-substitute:

\[ V_{n+1} = V_n - h\,\frac{U_n + hV_n}{1 + h^2} = \frac{(1 + h^2)\,V_n - hU_n - h^2V_n}{1 + h^2} = \frac{V_n - hU_n}{1 + h^2}. \]

So

\[ \mat G_B = (\eye - h\mat A)^{-1} = \frac{1}{1 + h^2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & h \\ -h & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{\mat G_F}{1 + h^2}, \]

and multiplying back, $\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & -h \\ h & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right]\mat G_B = \eye$ confirms it. The backward Euler point is the forward Euler point pulled in towards the origin by the factor $1/(1 + h^2)$.

Eigenvalues and energy

The eigenvalues of $\eye - h\mat A$ are $1 \mp ih$, and $\mat G_B$ is its inverse, so its eigenvalues are the reciprocals:

\[ \lambda = \frac{1}{1 \mp ih} = \frac{1 \pm ih}{1 + h^2}, \qquad \abs{\lambda} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + h^2}} < 1, \qquad \theta = \arctan h, \]

the same angle as forward Euler. For the energy, write the old point in terms of the new one, $U_n = U_{n+1} - hV_{n+1}$ and $V_n = V_{n+1} + hU_{n+1}$. The cross terms cancel as before:

\[ U_n^2 + V_n^2 = (U_{n+1} - hV_{n+1})^2 + (V_{n+1} + hU_{n+1})^2 = (1 + h^2)(U_{n+1}^2 + V_{n+1}^2). \]

So each step divides the energy by $1 + h^2$, and we end up with a decay. The geometry is that of forward Euler run backwards: the step is at right angles to the new radius, the line from the origin to the new point, so by Pythagoras the old point is the one farther from the origin.

Decaying problems, and the cost

For a decaying problem $\dot y = -ay$ with $a > 0$, backward Euler gives $Y_{n+1} = Y_n - ah\,Y_{n+1}$, so $Y_{n+1} = Y_n/(1 + ah)$: the computed solution decays at every step size. Forward Euler gives $Y_{n+1} = (1 - ah)\,Y_n$, which grows in size once $ah > 2$. Lecture 16 compares all the methods in this way. The price of backward Euler is the solve with $\eye - h\mat A$ at every step. For a large system with $m$ unknowns and a fixed $h$, factor $\eye - h\mat A = \mat L\mat U$ once (Lecture 4); each step is then a forward and a back substitution.

Example 2.1 (Backward Euler by hand). Given. The same oscillator and start as in Example 1.1, $\vec Y_0 = (1, 0)$, and a step $h$.

Find. What are $\vec Y_1 = \mat G_B\vec Y_0$ and $\vec Y_2 = \mat G_B^2\vec Y_0$ for a general $h$, and for $h = 0.5$? What happens to $U_n^2 + V_n^2$, and how far is $\vec Y_2$ from the true point at $t = 1$?

Answer. With $h$ as a letter,

\[ \vec Y_1 = \frac{1}{1 + h^2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & h \\ -h & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{1 + h^2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -h \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \vec Y_2 = \frac{\mat G_F^2\vec Y_0}{(1 + h^2)^2} = \frac{1}{(1 + h^2)^2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 - h^2 \\ -2h \end{bmatrix}, \]

using $\mat G_B = \mat G_F/(1 + h^2)$ and $\mat G_F^2\vec Y_0$ from Example 1.1. Now put $h = 0.5$, so $1 + h^2 = 1.25$:

\[ \mat G_B = \frac{1}{1.25}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0.5 \\ -0.5 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.8 & 0.4 \\ -0.4 & 0.8 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \vec Y_1 = \frac{1}{1.25}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -0.5 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \vec Y_2 = \frac{1}{1.5625}\begin{bmatrix} 0.75 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}, \]

that is $(U_1, V_1) = (0.8, -0.4)$ and $(U_2, V_2) = (0.48, -0.64)$. $U_n^2 + V_n^2$ goes $1, 0.8, 0.64$: it is divided by 1.25 at each step. Each point lies on the same ray as the forward Euler point of Example 1.1: $(0.8, -0.4) = 0.8\,(1, -0.5)$ and $(0.48, -0.64) = 0.64\,(0.75, -1)$. The true point $(0.540, -0.841)$ is 0.210 away.

Neither Euler method is safe for an oscillation

It is tempting to call backward Euler “the stable one” and stop. For a decaying problem that is often all we need. For an oscillation, damping the amplitude to zero is as wrong as blowing it up: after enough steps a bell that should still be ringing has gone silent. Stability, $\abs{\lambda} \le 1$, is necessary, but the oscillator wants $\abs{\lambda} = 1$.

3 · Leapfrog

One more choice of grid points, mixing old and new:

\[ \text{RHS} = \begin{bmatrix} V_n \\ -U_{n+1} \end{bmatrix} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \begin{cases} U_{n+1} = U_n + h\,V_n \\ V_{n+1} = V_n - h\,U_{n+1} \end{cases} \]

Update the position first, using the old velocity; then update the velocity, using the new position. The method is explicit even though $U_{n+1}$ appears on the right-hand side: by the time the second equation is used, $U_{n+1}$ is already known. Each step costs one evaluation of the force, the same as forward Euler. For a general force, $\ddot u = F(u)$, the updates are $U_{n+1} = U_n + h\,V_n$ and $V_{n+1} = V_n + h\,F(U_{n+1})$; for the oscillator $F(u) = -u$.

The mixed choice of grid points looks arbitrary, but it is what comes out of the second-order equation $\ddot u = F(u)$ when $\ddot u$ is replaced by a centred difference. That derivation also explains its accuracy, its step limit and what it conserves.

The centred second difference

Write the Taylor series of $u$ one step ahead and one step behind, with every derivative taken at $t_n$:

\[ \begin{aligned} u(t_n + h) &= u + h\,\dot u + \frac{h^2}{2}\,\ddot u + \frac{h^3}{6}\,\dddot u + \frac{h^4}{24}\,u^{(4)} + \frac{h^5}{120}\,u^{(5)} + \cdots, \\ u(t_n - h) &= u - h\,\dot u + \frac{h^2}{2}\,\ddot u - \frac{h^3}{6}\,\dddot u + \frac{h^4}{24}\,u^{(4)} - \frac{h^5}{120}\,u^{(5)} + \cdots . \end{aligned} \]

The two lines differ only in the signs of the odd powers of $h$. Add them: the terms $h\,\dot u$, $\frac{h^3}{6}\,\dddot u$ and $\frac{h^5}{120}u^{(5)}$ appear once with $+$ and once with $-$ and cancel, while the even powers are doubled:

\[ u(t_n + h) + u(t_n - h) = 2u + 2\cdot\frac{h^2}{2}\,\ddot u + 2\cdot\frac{h^4}{24}\,u^{(4)} + \cdots = 2u(t_n) + h^2\,\ddot u(t_n) + \frac{h^4}{12}\,u^{(4)}(t_n) + \cdots . \]

Subtract $2u(t_n)$ from both sides:

\[ u(t_{n+1}) - 2u(t_n) + u(t_{n-1}) = h^2\,\ddot u(t_n) + \frac{h^4}{12}\,u^{(4)}(t_n) + \cdots, \]

and divide by $h^2$:

\[ \frac{u(t_{n+1}) - 2u(t_n) + u(t_{n-1})}{h^2} = \ddot u(t_n) + \frac{h^2}{12}\,u^{(4)}(t_n) + \cdots . \]

This is the centred second difference of Lecture 3, now taken in time, and its error is of order $h^2$. For a quadratic $u(t)$ the fourth derivative is zero and the formula has no error at all; Example 3.1 uses this. Put the centred difference in place of $\ddot u$ in $\ddot u = -u$:

\[ U_{n+1} - 2U_n + U_{n-1} = -h^2\,U_n . \]

This is the two-step form of leapfrog: each new position comes from the two before it.

Velocities at half steps

To turn the two-step form into a one-step method, bring in the velocity between two positions,

\[ V_{n+1/2} = \frac{U_{n+1} - U_n}{h}. \]

How good a velocity is it? Expand about the midpoint $t_{n+1/2} = t_n + h/2$, now with every derivative taken at $t_{n+1/2}$:

\[ \begin{aligned} u(t_{n+1}) &= u\bigl(t_{n+1/2} + \tfrac h2\bigr) = u + \frac h2\,\dot u + \frac{h^2}{8}\,\ddot u + \frac{h^3}{48}\,\dddot u + \cdots, \\ u(t_n) &= u\bigl(t_{n+1/2} - \tfrac h2\bigr) = u - \frac h2\,\dot u + \frac{h^2}{8}\,\ddot u - \frac{h^3}{48}\,\dddot u + \cdots . \end{aligned} \]

This time subtract: the even powers cancel and the odd powers double,

\[ u(t_{n+1}) - u(t_n) = h\,\dot u(t_{n+1/2}) + \frac{h^3}{24}\,\dddot u(t_{n+1/2}) + \cdots, \]

and after dividing by $h$,

\[ \frac{u(t_{n+1}) - u(t_n)}{h} = \dot u(t_{n+1/2}) + \frac{h^2}{24}\,\dddot u(t_{n+1/2}) + \cdots . \]

So $V_{n+1/2}$ is a centred difference too: it approximates the velocity at the half step with an error of order $h^2$. In terms of area, the drift $U_{n+1} = U_n + h\,V_{n+1/2}$ is the midpoint rule for $u(t_{n+1}) - u(t_n) = \int_{t_n}^{t_{n+1}} v\,dt$ (sketch below).

Now write the left side of the two-step form as a difference of two such velocities:

\[ U_{n+1} - 2U_n + U_{n-1} = (U_{n+1} - U_n) - (U_n - U_{n-1}) = h\,V_{n+1/2} - h\,V_{n-1/2}. \]

The two-step form says that this equals $-h^2U_n$, and dividing by $h$,

\[ V_{n+1/2} = V_{n-1/2} - h\,U_n . \]

So the two-step form has split into

\[ U_{n+1} = U_n + h\,V_{n+1/2}, \qquad V_{n+1/2} = V_{n-1/2} - h\,U_n . \]

The first equation is the definition of $V_{n+1/2}$, and the second is the two-step form divided by $h$. Shift the second equation by one step ($n \to n + 1$),

\[ V_{n+3/2} = V_{n+1/2} - h\,U_{n+1}, \]

so that both updates go from step $n$ to step $n + 1$ in the order they are computed: first the new position $U_{n+1}$, then the velocity after it. Finally write $V_n$ for $V_{n+1/2}$, the velocity half a step after $t_n$ (and $V_{n+1}$ for $V_{n+3/2}$):

\[ U_{n+1} = U_n + h\,V_n, \qquad V_{n+1} = V_n - h\,U_{n+1}. \]

These are the leapfrog updates at the start of this section. Positions live at whole steps and velocities at half steps, and each update leaps over the other variable:

Start with half a kick

$V_0$ stands for the velocity at $t = h/2$. Expand $v$ about $t = 0$ and use $\dot v = \ddot u = -u$:

\[ v\bigl(\tfrac h2\bigr) = v(0) + \frac h2\,\dot v(0) + \frac{h^2}{8}\,\ddot v(0) + \cdots = v(0) - \frac h2\,u(0) + \Oh{h^2}. \]

So start with half a step of the force $-u$:

\[ V_0 = v(0) - \tfrac h2\,u(0). \]

With $V_0 = v(0)$ the method still runs and stays stable, but its error drops to first order. With 100 steps per period the largest position error over one period is 0.031 that way, against 0.00079 with the half kick. For a general force the half kick is $V_0 = v(0) + \frac h2\,F(u(0))$.

Example 3.1 (A thrown ball). Given. A ball thrown upwards, $\ddot u = -g$ with $g = 10\ \text{m/s}^2$, $u(0) = 0$, $v(0) = 10\ \text{m/s}$, stepped with leapfrog at $h = 0.5\ \text{s}$ and the half kick. The true height is $u(t) = 10t - 5t^2$.

Find. What are $V_0$ and $U_1, \dots, U_4$? How do they compare with $u(t)$? What does forward Euler give with the same step?

Answer. The force is $F = -g$, so the half kick gives $V_0 = 10 - 0.25\cdot 10 = 7.5$, and each step adds $h\,V_n$ to the position and $-hg = -5$ to the velocity:

\[ \begin{aligned} U_1 &= 0 + 0.5\cdot 7.5 = 3.75, & V_1 &= 7.5 - 5 = 2.5, \\ U_2 &= 3.75 + 0.5\cdot 2.5 = 5, & V_2 &= 2.5 - 5 = -2.5, \\ U_3 &= 5 + 0.5\cdot(-2.5) = 3.75, & V_3 &= -2.5 - 5 = -7.5, \\ U_4 &= 3.75 + 0.5\cdot(-7.5) = 0. & & \end{aligned} \]

The true heights are $u(0.5) = 3.75$, $u(1) = 5$, $u(1.5) = 3.75$ and $u(2) = 0$: leapfrog has no error at all. The half kick is exact here, because $v = 10 - 10t$ is linear in $t$: $V_0 = v(h/2)$, so $U_1 = u(h)$. From then on the centred second difference, which is exact for a quadratic, keeps every $U_n$ exact, and each $V_n$ is the true velocity at the half step (7.5 at $t = 0.25$, 2.5 at $t = 0.75$, and so on). Forward Euler makes the same two updates, $U_{n+1} = U_n + hV_n$ and $V_{n+1} = V_n - hg$ (for a constant force the old and new positions give the same force), but it starts from $V_0 = v(0) = 10$, the velocity at $t = 0$ rather than at $h/2$. It gives $U = 5, 7.5, 7.5, 5$ at $t = 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2$: one step puts it $gh^2/2 = 1.25$ m too high, the error grows by that much at every step, and after $n$ steps it is $1.25\,n$ m, an error of $gh\,t/2$. It lands 5 m above the ground when the ball is back at $u = 0$. For a constant force the half kick is the whole difference.

The growth matrix

Back to the oscillator. Put the first update into the second:

\[ V_{n+1} = V_n - h\,U_{n+1} = V_n - h\,(U_n + hV_n) = -h\,U_n + (1 - h^2)\,V_n . \]

So

\[ \mat G_L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & h \\ -h & 1 - h^2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -h & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & h \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \]

a drift (the position moves with the velocity) followed by a kick (the velocity changes with the force). Each factor has determinant 1, so $\det\mat G_L = 1$ (directly, $1\cdot(1 - h^2) - h\cdot(-h) = 1$), and $\operatorname{tr}\mat G_L = 2 - h^2$.

Eigenvalues and the step limit

The characteristic polynomial is

\[ \det(\mat G_L - \lambda\eye) = (1 - \lambda)(1 - h^2 - \lambda) + h^2 = \lambda^2 - (2 - h^2)\,\lambda + 1 . \]

The two-step form gives the same polynomial: put $U_n = \lambda^n$ into $U_{n+1} - 2U_n + U_{n-1} = -h^2U_n$ and divide by $\lambda^{n-1}$ to get $\lambda^2 - 2\lambda + 1 = -h^2\lambda$. The roots are

\[ \lambda = \frac{(2 - h^2) \pm \sqrt{(2 - h^2)^2 - 4}}{2}, \qquad (2 - h^2)^2 - 4 = h^2(h^2 - 4), \]

with product 1 and sum $2 - h^2$. There are three cases.

  • $h < 2$: the square root is imaginary, and $\lambda = 1 - \frac{h^2}{2} \pm ih\sqrt{1 - \frac{h^2}{4}}$ are complex conjugates with \[ \abs{\lambda}^2 = \Bigl(1 - \frac{h^2}{2}\Bigr)^2 + h^2\Bigl(1 - \frac{h^2}{4}\Bigr) = 1 - h^2 + \frac{h^4}{4} + h^2 - \frac{h^4}{4} = 1 . \] For every $h < 2$ the computed oscillation neither grows nor decays.
  • $h = 2$: the double root $\lambda = -1$. The solutions are $U_n = (c + dn)(-1)^n$, which grow in proportion to $n$.
  • $h > 2$: the roots are real, with product 1 and sum $2 - h^2 < -2$. Both are negative and one of them is below $-1$, so the method blows up.

The step limit $h < 2$ means more than $\pi$ steps per period. Inside the limit $\lambda + \bar\lambda = 2\cos\theta = 2 - h^2$, so

\[ \cos\theta = 1 - \frac{h^2}{2}, \qquad 2\sin^2\frac\theta2 = 1 - \cos\theta = \frac{h^2}{2}, \qquad \theta = 2\arcsin\frac h2 = h + \frac{h^3}{24} + \cdots, \]

a little more than $h$: the computed oscillation runs slightly ahead of the true one.

For the masses and springs of last time, $\ddot{\vec u} + \mat K\vec u = \vec 0$ with $\mat M = \eye$, write $\mat K = \mat Q\mat\Lambda\mat Q\T$ and $\vec u = \mat Q\vec x$ as in Lecture 14. Each normal mode obeys $\ddot x_j + \omega_j^2x_j = 0$, where $\omega_j^2$ is the $j$th eigenvalue of $\mat K$. Leapfrog is linear, so in these coordinates it acts on each $x_j$ separately, and on $x_j$ it is leapfrog on our oscillator with the step $h\omega_j$ (rescale the velocity by $\omega_j$). So it is stable when

\[ h\,\omega_{\max} < 2 . \]

The fastest mode sets the step, even when it carries almost none of the motion. Molecular dynamics codes use leapfrog (in the form called the Verlet method), and their steps of one or two femtoseconds come from the fastest motion: a bond to a hydrogen atom vibrates with a period near 10 fs, which puts the stability limit near 3 fs, and accuracy asks for less. Explicit crash simulations of cars are limited in the same way by their smallest and stiffest elements.

What leapfrog conserves

For the true motion, multiplying $\ddot u + u = 0$ by $\dot u$ gives $\frac12\frac{d}{dt}(\dot u^2 + u^2) = 0$: the energy is constant. Do the same to the two-step form, with $U_{n+1} - U_{n-1}$ ($2h$ times a centred velocity) in the role of $\dot u$:

\[ (U_{n+1} - 2U_n + U_{n-1})(U_{n+1} - U_{n-1}) = -h^2\,U_n(U_{n+1} - U_{n-1}). \]

On the left, $U_{n+1} - 2U_n + U_{n-1} = (U_{n+1} - U_n) - (U_n - U_{n-1})$ and $U_{n+1} - U_{n-1} = (U_{n+1} - U_n) + (U_n - U_{n-1})$, so the product is a difference of squares,

\[ (U_{n+1} - U_n)^2 - (U_n - U_{n-1})^2 = h^2\bigl(V_{n+1/2}^2 - V_{n-1/2}^2\bigr). \]

The right side is $-h^2(U_nU_{n+1} - U_{n-1}U_n)$. Divide by $h^2$ and collect:

\[ V_{n+1/2}^2 + U_nU_{n+1} = V_{n-1/2}^2 + U_{n-1}U_n . \]

So this quantity does not change from step to step. In the notation of the updates ($V_n$ for $V_{n+1/2}$, and $U_{n+1} = U_n + hV_n$) it is

\[ Q_n = U_nU_{n+1} + V_n^2 = U_n^2 + h\,U_nV_n + V_n^2 . \]

Leapfrog does not keep $U^2 + V^2$ ($\mat G_L$ is not orthogonal), but it keeps this nearby quantity. The points $(U_n, V_n)$ lie on the ellipse $U^2 + hUV + V^2 = Q_0$: a circle squeezed along the diagonal $u = v$ and stretched along $u = -v$, which becomes the circle as $h \to 0$. The energy error stays bounded however many periods we compute; it does not drift.

Example 3.2 (Leapfrog by hand). Given. $\ddot u + u = 0$ with $u(0) = 1$, $v(0) = 0$, the step $h = 0.5$, and the half-kick start.

Find. What is $\vec Y_0$? What are $\vec Y_1 = \mat G_L\vec Y_0$ and $\vec Y_2 = \mat G_L\vec Y_1$? Does $U_n^2 + V_n^2$ stay fixed? Does $Q_n$?

Answer. With $h$ as a letter, $\vec Y_0 = (1, -h/2)$ and

\[ \vec Y_1 = \mat G_L\vec Y_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & h \\ -h & 1 - h^2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -h/2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 - h^2/2 \\ -h - (1 - h^2)\,h/2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 - h^2/2 \\ -(3h - h^3)/2 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Now put $h = 0.5$:

\[ \mat G_L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0.5 \\ -0.5 & 0.75 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \vec Y_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -0.25 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \vec Y_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 0.875 \\ -0.6875 \end{bmatrix}, \] \[ \vec Y_2 = \mat G_L\vec Y_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 0.875 - 0.34375 \\ -0.4375 - 0.515625 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.53125 \\ -0.953125 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Step by step this is $U_1 = 1 + 0.5\,(-0.25) = 0.875$, $V_1 = -0.25 - 0.5\,(0.875) = -0.6875$, $U_2 = 0.875 + 0.5\,(-0.6875) = 0.53125$ and $V_2 = -0.6875 - 0.5\,(0.53125) = -0.953125$. $U_2$ should be compared with $u(1) = 0.540$ and $V_2$ with the velocity at the half step, $v(1.25) = -\sin 1.25 = -0.949$: together they are off by 0.010. The sum $U_n^2 + V_n^2$ goes $1.0625, 1.2383, 1.1907$ and wanders. But $Q_n = U_n^2 + 0.5\,U_nV_n + V_n^2 = 0.9375$ at every step, which is $1 - h^2/4$. The two-step form holds as well: $U_2 - 2U_1 + U_0 = -0.21875 = -h^2U_1$.

Example 3.3 (Above the step limit). Given. Leapfrog for $\ddot u + u = 0$ with $h = 2.5$.

Find. What are the eigenvalues of $\mat G_L$? What happens to the computed solution?

Answer. $\operatorname{tr}\mat G_L = 2 - 6.25 = -4.25$ and $\det\mat G_L = 1$, so $\lambda^2 + 4.25\,\lambda + 1 = 0$ and $\lambda = -4$ or $\lambda = -\frac14$. For $\lambda = -4$,

\[ \mat G_L + 4\eye = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 2.5 \\ -2.5 & -1.25 \end{bmatrix} \qquad \text{has null vector} \qquad \vec x = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}. \]

The part of $\vec Y_0$ along $\vec x$ is multiplied by $-4$ at every step: after ten steps by $4^{10} \approx 10^6$, with the sign changing at each step. Nothing in the code signals the problem; the numbers grow.

4 · The trapezoidal method

The set-up again

Recall the notation. The step is $h$ and $t_n = nh$. The vector $\vec Y_n = (U_n, V_n)$ is the computed approximation to $\vec y(t_n) = (u(t_n), v(t_n))$. The oscillator is $\dot{\vec y} = \vec f(t, \vec y)$ with $\vec f = \mat A\vec y = (v, -u)$ and $\mat A = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$. Over one step the exact solution satisfies

\[ \vec y(t_{n+1}) = \vec y(t_n) + \int_{t_n}^{t_{n+1}} \vec f(t, \vec y(t))\,dt . \]

Forward Euler replaced the integral by the rectangle $h\,\vec f(t_n, \vec Y_n)$, with the height at the left end. Backward Euler used $h\,\vec f(t_{n+1}, \vec Y_{n+1})$, the height at the right end. The trapezoidal method uses the trapezoid, the average of the two heights:

\[ \vec Y_{n+1} = \vec Y_n + \frac h2\Bigl(\vec f(t_n, \vec Y_n) + \vec f(t_{n+1}, \vec Y_{n+1})\Bigr). \]

Its right-hand side is the average of the forward and backward Euler right-hand sides, and like backward Euler it is implicit.

Why the average is better

Taylor's theorem compares the difference quotient with the average of the two end slopes. Expand $u(t_{n+1})$ and $\dot u(t_{n+1})$ about $t_n$ (every derivative at $t_n$):

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{u(t_{n+1}) - u(t_n)}{h} &= \dot u + \frac h2\,\ddot u + \frac{h^2}{6}\,\dddot u + \cdots, \\ \frac{\dot u(t_n) + \dot u(t_{n+1})}{2} &= \frac{\dot u + \bigl(\dot u + h\,\ddot u + \frac{h^2}{2}\,\dddot u + \cdots\bigr)}{2} = \dot u + \frac h2\,\ddot u + \frac{h^2}{4}\,\dddot u + \cdots . \end{aligned} \]

The first two terms agree, and the difference starts at $h^2$:

\[ \frac{u(t_{n+1}) - u(t_n)}{h} = \frac{\dot u(t_n) + \dot u(t_{n+1})}{2} - \frac{h^2}{12}\,\dddot u(t_n) + \cdots . \]

The difference quotient matches the average of the two end slopes with an error of order $h^2$, one order better than either end alone (the $\pm\frac h2\ddot u$ errors of the two Euler methods cancel). So the trapezoidal method is second-order accurate. The sketch below shows the same cancellation with areas.

The trapezoidal method on the oscillator

Evaluate the right-hand side as the average of the old and new time levels:

\[ \text{RHS} = \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{V_n + V_{n+1}}{2} \\[2ex] -\dfrac{U_n + U_{n+1}}{2} \end{bmatrix} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \begin{cases} U_{n+1} = U_n + \frac h2\,(V_n + V_{n+1}) \\ V_{n+1} = V_n - \frac h2\,(U_n + U_{n+1}) \end{cases} \]

The energy first. Write the first equation as $U_{n+1} - U_n = \frac h2\,(V_n + V_{n+1})$ and multiply it by $U_n + U_{n+1}$; write the second as $V_{n+1} - V_n = -\frac h2\,(U_n + U_{n+1})$ and multiply it by $V_n + V_{n+1}$:

\[ U_{n+1}^2 - U_n^2 = \tfrac h2\,(V_n + V_{n+1})(U_n + U_{n+1}), \qquad V_{n+1}^2 - V_n^2 = -\tfrac h2\,(U_n + U_{n+1})(V_n + V_{n+1}). \]

The right sides cancel when added, so $U_{n+1}^2 + V_{n+1}^2 = U_n^2 + V_n^2$ at every step and for every $h$. This is the discrete copy of $\frac{d}{dt}(u^2 + v^2) = 2uv + 2v(-u) = 0$, with $u$ and $v$ replaced by the averages of old and new values.

For the growth matrix, collect the new time level on the left and the old on the right:

\[ \begin{cases} U_{n+1} - \frac h2\,V_{n+1} = U_n + \frac h2\,V_n \\ \frac h2\,U_{n+1} + V_{n+1} = V_n - \frac h2\,U_n \end{cases} \qquad \text{that is} \qquad \Bigl(\eye - \frac h2\mat A\Bigr)\vec Y_{n+1} = \Bigl(\eye + \frac h2\mat A\Bigr)\vec Y_n, \]

so

\[ \mat G_T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -\frac h2 \\ \frac h2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & \frac h2 \\ -\frac h2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{1 + \frac{h^2}{4}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & \frac h2 \\ -\frac h2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}^2 = \frac{1}{1 + \frac{h^2}{4}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 - \frac{h^2}{4} & h \\ -h & 1 - \frac{h^2}{4} \end{bmatrix}. \]

The middle step used the fact that the inverse of $\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & -h/2 \\ h/2 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$ is $\frac{1}{1 + h^2/4}\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & h/2 \\ -h/2 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$, a multiple of the matrix it is being multiplied by.

The columns of the result confirm the energy argument. Each has squared length

\[ \frac{\bigl(1 - \frac{h^2}{4}\bigr)^2 + h^2}{\bigl(1 + \frac{h^2}{4}\bigr)^2} = \frac{1 - \frac{h^2}{2} + \frac{h^4}{16} + h^2}{\bigl(1 + \frac{h^2}{4}\bigr)^2} = \frac{\bigl(1 + \frac{h^2}{4}\bigr)^2}{\bigl(1 + \frac{h^2}{4}\bigr)^2} = 1, \]

and the two columns are orthogonal. So $\mat G_T$ is an orthogonal matrix, a pure rotation, and $\vec Y_n = \mat G_T^n\vec Y_0$ keeps the length of $\vec Y_0$ for every $h$.

Both factors of $\mat G_T$ are built from $\mat A$, so they share its eigenvectors, and an eigenvalue $\mu = \pm i$ of $\mat A$ becomes $(1 + h\mu/2)/(1 - h\mu/2)$:

\[ \lambda = \frac{1 \pm ih/2}{1 \mp ih/2}, \qquad \abs{\lambda} = 1, \qquad \theta = \arg\Bigl(1 + \frac{ih}{2}\Bigr) - \arg\Bigl(1 - \frac{ih}{2}\Bigr) = 2\arctan\frac h2 = h - \frac{h^3}{12} + \cdots . \]

The numerator and denominator are complex conjugates, which is why $\abs{\lambda} = 1$. The computed oscillation keeps its amplitude and falls slightly behind, at twice the rate at which leapfrog runs ahead. The cost is one solve per step, as for backward Euler.

The order shows in the growth matrix too. Write $\mat X = \frac h2\mat A$ and expand the inverse in a series (for small $h$):

\[ \begin{aligned} (\eye - \mat X)^{-1} &= \eye + \mat X + \mat X^2 + \mat X^3 + \cdots, \\ \mat G_T &= (\eye + \mat X + \mat X^2 + \mat X^3 + \cdots)(\eye + \mat X) = \eye + 2\mat X + 2\mat X^2 + 2\mat X^3 + \cdots \\ &= \eye + h\mat A + \frac{(h\mat A)^2}{2} + \frac{(h\mat A)^3}{4} + \cdots, \end{aligned} \]

which agrees with $e^{h\mat A} = \eye + h\mat A + (h\mat A)^2/2 + (h\mat A)^3/6 + \cdots$ up to the square and differs by $(h\mat A)^3/12$: second order again. Applied to the heat equation the trapezoidal method is called the Crank–Nicolson method. It is also the default integration method of SPICE circuit simulators.

Example 4.1 (The trapezoidal method by hand). Given. $\ddot u + u = 0$ with $u(0) = 1$, $v(0) = 0$, and $h = 0.5$.

Find. What is $\mat G_T$? What are $(U_1, V_1)$ and $(U_2, V_2)$? How does the angle per step compare with $h$?

Answer. With $h$ as a letter, $\vec Y_1 = \mat G_T\vec Y_0 = \dfrac{1}{1 + h^2/4}\,(1 - h^2/4,\ -h)$, the first column of $\mat G_T$. For $h = 0.5$, $1 + h^2/4 = 17/16$ and $1 - h^2/4 = 15/16$, so

\[ \mat G_T = \frac{16}{17}\begin{bmatrix} \frac{15}{16} & \frac12 \\ -\frac12 & \frac{15}{16} \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{17}\begin{bmatrix} 15 & 8 \\ -8 & 15 \end{bmatrix}. \]

The columns are unit vectors because $8^2 + 15^2 = 17^2$. Then

\[ (U_1, V_1) = \Bigl(\tfrac{15}{17}, -\tfrac{8}{17}\Bigr) = (0.882, -0.471), \qquad (U_2, V_2) = \Bigl(\tfrac{161}{289}, -\tfrac{240}{289}\Bigr) = (0.557, -0.830), \]

and $U_n^2 + V_n^2 = 1$ at every step ($161^2 + 240^2 = 289^2$). The angle per step is $\arctan(8/15) = 0.4900$ against $h = 0.5$, behind by 0.0100 per step, close to $h^3/12 = 0.0104$. The true point at $t = 1$ is 0.020 away, and all of that is phase error.

Structure, not just accuracy

Forward Euler grows, backward Euler decays, the trapezoidal method does neither. The reason is not that the trapezoidal method is more accurate in a Taylor-series sense. It is that its growth matrix is orthogonal, and orthogonal matrices preserve length. Leapfrog, which is explicit, keeps a nearby quadratic quantity instead. Choosing a scheme whose discrete operator inherits the structure of the continuous one (here, conservation of energy) is a different design principle from chasing one more order of $h$, and often the more useful one.

Fast decaying modes ring

For a fast decay $\dot y = -ay$ the trapezoidal method multiplies by $(1 - ah/2)/(1 + ah/2)$ at each step, which is close to $-1$ when $ah$ is large. So it barely damps such a mode: it changes the sign of the mode at every step and shrinks it only a little. Backward Euler multiplies by $1/(1 + ah)$, close to 0, and removes the mode within a step or two. In circuit simulators that use the trapezoidal method this shows up as ringing after a sudden switch.

Each method replaces the integral of $\vec f$ over one step by an area, and each area misses part of the true one. The examples of this lecture all take two steps of $h = 0.5$ from $(u, v) = (1, 0)$.

Where this is going

Next time we take the last two rows of the table further. A weighted average of the midpoint and trapezoid areas cancels their $h^3$ errors; the result is Simpson's rule, which is fourth order. Turning Simpson's rule into a time stepper for $\dot{\vec y} = \vec f(t, \vec y)$ gives the fourth-order Runge–Kutta method (RK4). Then we compare all five methods: accuracy for the work, stability regions, and problems with fast decaying modes.

Appendix: the four methods in Python

The script below builds the four growth matrices, repeats the hand calculations of Examples 1.1 to 4.1, checks leapfrog's conserved quantity and its step limit, and follows the thrown ball of Example 3.1. It needs only NumPy.

"""Lecture 15: four time-stepping methods for u'' + u = 0, as y' = Ay with y = (u, v)."""
import numpy as np

A = np.array([[0.0, 1.0], [-1.0, 0.0]])
I = np.eye(2)
methods = ["forward Euler", "backward Euler", "leapfrog", "trapezoidal"]

def growth_matrix(method, h):
    if method == "forward Euler":
        return I + h * A
    if method == "backward Euler":
        return np.linalg.inv(I - h * A)    # a real code solves with I - hA instead
    if method == "leapfrog":
        return np.array([[1.0, h], [-h, 1.0 - h * h]])
    if method == "trapezoidal":
        return np.linalg.solve(I - h * A / 2, I + h * A / 2)

def start(method, h):            # u(0) = 1, v(0) = 0; leapfrog gets half a kick
    return np.array([1.0, -h / 2 if method == "leapfrog" else 0.0])

def distance(method, y, t, h):   # leapfrog's V is the velocity half a step later
    s = h / 2 if method == "leapfrog" else 0.0
    return np.hypot(y[0] - np.cos(t), y[1] + np.sin(t + s))

# 1. two steps of h = 0.5, and the size and angle of the eigenvalues of G
h = 0.5
print("two steps of h = 0.5 from u = 1, v = 0:")
for m in methods:
    G = growth_matrix(m, h)
    y = G @ G @ start(m, h)
    lam = np.linalg.eigvals(G)[0]
    print(f"  {m:14s} U2 {y[0]:8.5f}  V2 {y[1]:8.5f}  off {distance(m, y, 1.0, h):.5f}"
          f"  |lambda| {abs(lam):.5f}  angle {abs(np.angle(lam)):.5f}")

# 2. leapfrog keeps Q = U^2 + hUV + V^2, and fails above h = 2
y = start("leapfrog", h)
Q = []
for n in range(6):
    Q.append(y[0] ** 2 + h * y[0] * y[1] + y[1] ** 2)
    y = growth_matrix("leapfrog", h) @ y
print("leapfrog Q at h = 0.5:", np.round(Q, 12))
print("leapfrog eigenvalues at h = 2.5:",
      np.sort(np.linalg.eigvals(growth_matrix("leapfrog", 2.5)).real))

# 2b. leapfrog on a thrown ball, u'' = -g: with the half kick it has no error at all
g, hb = 10.0, 0.5
U, V = 0.0, 10.0 - hb / 2 * g                  # V0 = v(0) - (h/2) g
for n in range(1, 5):
    U = U + hb * V                             # drift
    V = V - hb * g                             # kick
    t = n * hb
    print(f"  ball t = {t:.1f}  U = {U:5.2f}  true {10 * t - 5 * t * t:5.2f}")
