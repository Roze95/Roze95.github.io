Lecture 12

Least Squares

Wednesday, October 21, 2026 MW 9:30–11:00, 6-120 Reading: Strang §2.3

Download PDF

After this lecture you should be able to
  • Set up a data-fitting problem as an overdetermined system $\mat X\vec k = \vec y$ and write the total squared error as $\vec e\T\vec e$.
  • Interpret least squares geometrically: find the point of $\operatorname{col}(\mat X)$ closest to $\vec y$, so that the residual is orthogonal to $\operatorname{col}(\mat X)$.
  • Turn that right angle into the normal equations $\mat X\T\mat X\,\overline{\vec k} = \mat X\T\vec y$, and solve them.
  • Extend the same matrix set-up to several independent variables and to polynomial models.

1 · A problem you have already met

Suppose we are handed data

\[ \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i\in\{1,\dots,m\}}, \qquad x_i, y_i \in \R . \]

For example, $x_i$ is a person's height and $y_i$ is their shoe size; or $x_i$ is the horsepower of a vehicle and $y_i$ is its cost; etc., etc. We want to fit a linear model

\[ y = kx + b \]

to that data.

How do we determine the parameters $k$ and $b$ of the line? No line passes through all the crosses — with $m$ data points and two unknowns the system is overdetermined — so “solve $\mat X\vec k = \vec y$” is the wrong question. We must decide what “best” means first.

2 · The classic criterion: minimise the total squared error

For a given data point $(x_i, y_i)$, the prediction of our model at $x_i$ is $kx_i + b$. The error between the model prediction and the actual observation $y_i$ is

\[ y_i - (kx_i + b), \]

and the total squared error is

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{m} \bigl[\, y_i - (kx_i + b) \,\bigr]^2 . \]

Let us write this using vectors and matrices. Collect the data into

\[ \mat X = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 & 1 \\ \vdots & \vdots \\ x_m & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \vec y = \begin{bmatrix} y_1 \\ \vdots \\ y_m \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \vec k = \begin{bmatrix} k \\ b \end{bmatrix}. \]

(The board writes these $X$, $Y$, $K$; the lower case here only marks which are vectors.) The column of ones is what carries the intercept $b$. The error as a vector is then

\[ \vec e = \vec y - \mat X\vec k, \qquad e_i = y_i - (kx_i + b), \]

and the total squared error is $\vec e\T\vec e = \sum_i e_i^2$:

\[ \vec e\T\vec e = (\vec y - \mat X\vec k)\T(\vec y - \mat X\vec k) = \vec y\T\vec y + \vec k\T\mat X\T\mat X\vec k - \vec y\T\mat X\vec k - \vec k\T\mat X\T\vec y . \]

The last two terms are $1\times 1$, hence equal to their own transposes, so they combine:

\[ \boxed{\ \vec e\T\vec e = \vec k\T(\mat X\T\mat X)\vec k - 2\,\vec y\T\mat X\vec k + \vec y\T\vec y\ } \]

So the least squares fitting problem can be written as the optimisation

\[ \min_{\vec k\in\R^2}\ \vec k\T(\mat X\T\mat X)\vec k - 2\,\vec y\T\mat X\vec k + \vec y\T\vec y . \]
Key idea

Everything about the data has been compressed into the two objects $\mat X\T\mat X$ (a $2\times 2$ matrix) and $\mat X\T\vec y$ (a 2-vector). The number of data points $m$ has left the problem. That collapse from $m$ to $2$ is the whole reason least squares is cheap.

Example 2.1 (Five points and a first guess). Given. Five data points

\[ (x_i, y_i) = (1,3),\ (3,7),\ (4,9),\ (5,16),\ (7,20) \]

and the model $y = kx + b$. The first three points lie exactly on the line $y = 2x + 1$, so that line is a natural first guess. (The data and the lines are drawn in Example 3.1.)

Find. (a) What are $\mat X$, $\vec y$ and $\vec k$ for these data? (b) What is the total squared error $\vec e\T\vec e$ of the guess $y = 2x + 1$? (c) What are $\mat X\T\mat X$, $\mat X\T\vec y$ and $\vec y\T\vec y$, and does the boxed formula give the same error for the guess?

Answer. (a) One row per data point, with the column of ones last:

\[ \mat X = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 3 & 1 \\ 4 & 1 \\ 5 & 1 \\ 7 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \vec y = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 7 \\ 9 \\ 16 \\ 20 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \vec k = \begin{bmatrix} k \\ b \end{bmatrix}. \]

There are five equations and two unknowns, and no choice of $k$ and $b$ satisfies all five: the first three points force $k = 2$ and $b = 1$, and that line misses $(5, 16)$.

(b) The guess has $\vec k = (2, 1)$ and predicts $2x_i + 1 = 3, 7, 9, 11, 15$. The errors are

\[ \vec e = \vec y - \mat X\vec k = (0,\,0,\,0,\,5,\,5), \qquad \vec e\T\vec e = 0 + 0 + 0 + 25 + 25 = 50 . \]

The guess fits three points exactly and misses the other two by 5 each.

(c) The entries are sums over the data, with $m = 5$:

\[ \mat X\T\mat X = \begin{bmatrix} \sum x_i^2 & \sum x_i \\ \sum x_i & m \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 100 & 20 \\ 20 & 5 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mat X\T\vec y = \begin{bmatrix} \sum x_i y_i \\ \sum y_i \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 280 \\ 55 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \vec y\T\vec y = \sum y_i^2 = 795 . \]

For $\vec k = (2, 1)$,

\[ \vec k\T(\mat X\T\mat X)\vec k = 100\cdot 4 + 2\cdot 20\cdot 2\cdot 1 + 5\cdot 1 = 485, \qquad \vec y\T\mat X\vec k = 280\cdot 2 + 55\cdot 1 = 615, \]

so the box gives $485 - 2\cdot 615 + 795 = 50$, as in (b). As a function of $\vec k$, the error depends on the data only through these three quantities. The constant $\vec y\T\vec y$ changes the smallest value of the error, not the $\vec k$ where it occurs.

The next section finds the minimum from a picture.

3 · A geometrical interpretation

Consider the vectors $\vec e$, $\vec y$, and $\mat X\vec k$: they all live in an $m$-dimensional Euclidean space $\R^m$ — one coordinate per data point, not per parameter. The total squared error $\vec e\T\vec e$ is just the squared Euclidean distance between $\vec y$ and $\mat X\vec k$.

Specifically, since $\mat X\vec k\in\operatorname{col}(\mat X)$ for every choice of $\vec k$, least squares fitting is finding the point in $\operatorname{col}(\mat X)$ closest to $\vec y$. The column space here is the plane spanned by the two columns $(x_1,\dots,x_m)\T$ and $(1,\dots,1)\T$ — a 2-dimensional slice of $\R^m$, and $\vec y$ almost certainly does not lie in it.

The one picture to remember

Least squares is not really about lines and data. It is: given a subspace and a point off it, drop a perpendicular. Every formula below is bookkeeping for that perpendicular.

From the diagram, the optimal $\vec k$, which we denote $\overline{\vec k}$, is such that

\[ \vec y - \mat X\overline{\vec k} \quad \text{is orthogonal to} \quad \operatorname{col}(\mat X). \]

The normal equations

Orthogonal to $\operatorname{col}(\mat X)$ means orthogonal to every column of $\mat X$. The columns of $\mat X$ are the rows of $\mat X\T$, so the right angle in the picture reads

\[ \mat X\T(\vec y - \mat X\overline{\vec k}) = \vec 0, \]

that is,

\[ \boxed{\ \mat X\T\mat X\,\overline{\vec k} = \mat X\T\vec y\ } \qquad \text{(the normal equations).} \]

They are built from the same $\mat X\T\mat X$ and $\mat X\T\vec y$ that appeared in Section 2. When the columns of $\mat X$ are independent, $\mat X\vec v\neq\vec 0$ for every $\vec v\neq\vec 0$, so $\vec v\T\mat X\T\mat X\vec v = \norm{\mat X\vec v}^2 > 0$. Then $\mat X\T\mat X$ is symmetric positive definite, hence invertible, and $\overline{\vec k} = (\mat X\T\mat X)\inv\mat X\T\vec y$. For a line this fails only when all the $x_i$ are equal.

Example 3.1 (The best line). Given. The data of Example 2.1 and the sums from its part (c): $\mat X\T\mat X$ has rows $(100, 20)$ and $(20, 5)$, and $\mat X\T\vec y = (280, 55)$.

Find. (a) Which line do the normal equations give? (b) Is the residual orthogonal to $\operatorname{col}(\mat X)$, and how does its squared length compare with the error 50 of the guess $y = 2x + 1$?

Answer. (a) The two equations are $100\,\bar k + 20\,\bar b = 280$ and $20\,\bar k + 5\,\bar b = 55$. Subtract $\tfrac15$ of the first from the second: $(5 - 4)\,\bar b = 55 - 56$, so $\bar b = -1$. Then $100\,\bar k = 280 + 20 = 300$, so $\bar k = 3$. The best line is $y = 3x - 1$.

(b) The fitted values are $3x_i - 1 = 2, 8, 11, 14, 20$. The residual is the error at the best $\overline{\vec k}$, which we write $\overline{\vec e} = \vec y - \mat X\overline{\vec k} = (1, -1, -2, 2, 0)$. Against the two columns $(1, 3, 4, 5, 7)$ and $(1, 1, 1, 1, 1)$ of $\mat X$,

\[ 1 - 3 - 8 + 10 + 0 = 0, \qquad 1 - 1 - 2 + 2 + 0 = 0, \]

so $\overline{\vec e}$ is orthogonal to both columns, and hence to every combination of them, which is all of $\operatorname{col}(\mat X)$. Its squared length is $\overline{\vec e}\T\overline{\vec e} = 1 + 1 + 4 + 4 + 0 = 10$, against 50 for the guess.

The vertical segments are the entries of $\overline{\vec e}$ (the one at $x = 7$ is zero). The dashed guess passes through three points and still has the larger error.

4 · Generalisations

Multivariate linear regression

Say our data has $n$ independent variables,

\[ \bigl\{(x_{i1}, x_{i2}, \dots, x_{in}\,;\, y_i)\bigr\}_{i\in\{1,\dots,m\}} . \]

Then our $\mat X$ matrix and $\vec k$ vector simply become larger:

\[ \mat X = \begin{pmatrix} x_{11} & x_{12} & \cdots & x_{1n} & 1 \\ x_{21} & x_{22} & \cdots & x_{2n} & 1 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots \\ x_{m1} & x_{m2} & \cdots & x_{mn} & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \vec k = \begin{pmatrix} k_1 \\ k_2 \\ \vdots \\ k_n \\ b \end{pmatrix}, \]

with $\mat X\in\R^{m\times(n+1)}$ and $\vec k\in\R^{n+1}$. Nothing else changes: the same two boxed formulas hold verbatim.

Nonlinear regression

For example, what happens if I want to fit a polynomial model

\[ y = \ell x^2 + mx + b \]

with parameters $\ell, m, b$? Work it out in a problem set!

“Linear” means linear in the parameters

The model $y = \ell x^2 + mx + b$ is a curve, but the unknowns $\ell, m, b$ still enter linearly — so it is still a least squares problem, with the columns of $\mat X$ now $(x_i^2)$, $(x_i)$, and $(1)$. Least squares does not care what functions of the data you put in the columns, only that the parameters multiply them linearly.

Example 4.1 (Kinetic energy of a glider on springs). Given. A 0.50 kg glider on a level air track is attached to two springs, one fixed at each end, and oscillates along the track. Photogates at five positions $x$ measure its speed as it passes, and each speed is converted to kinetic energy $y$:

position $x$ (cm)$-20$$-10$$0$$10$$20$
kinetic energy $y$ (mJ)274673887861680

With no friction, energy is conserved: $y + \tfrac12\kappa\,(x - x_0)^2 = \tfrac12\kappa A^2$, where $\kappa$ is the combined constant of the two springs, $x_0$ the equilibrium position and $A$ the amplitude. So $y$ is a parabola in $x$, opening downwards.

Find. (a) What are $\mat X$ and the normal equations for the model $y = \ell x^2 + mx + b$? (Here $m$ is the coefficient of $x$, not the number of points.) (b) Which parabola fits best? (c) What spring constant $\kappa$, equilibrium position $x_0$ and amplitude $A$ does it give?

Answer. (a) The columns are $x_i^2$, $x_i$ and $1$, and the unknowns are $(\ell, m, b)$:

\[ \mat X = \begin{bmatrix} 400 & -20 & 1 \\ 100 & -10 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 100 & 10 & 1 \\ 400 & 20 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \vec y = \begin{bmatrix} 274 \\ 673 \\ 887 \\ 861 \\ 680 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Call the three columns of $\mat X$

\[ \vec c_1 = (400, 100, 0, 100, 400), \qquad \vec c_2 = (-20, -10, 0, 10, 20), \qquad \vec c_3 = (1, 1, 1, 1, 1). \]

They are the rows of $\mat X\T$:

\[ \mat X\T = \begin{bmatrix} 400 & 100 & 0 & 100 & 400 \\ -20 & -10 & 0 & 10 & 20 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{matrix} \leftarrow \vec c_1\T \\ \leftarrow \vec c_2\T \\ \leftarrow \vec c_3\T \end{matrix} \]

Computing $\mat X\T\mat X$. Entry $(i, j)$ is row $i$ of $\mat X\T$ times column $j$ of $\mat X$, which is the dot product $\vec c_i\T\vec c_j$ of two columns of $\mat X$:

\[ \mat X\T\mat X = \begin{bmatrix} \vec c_1\T\vec c_1 & \vec c_1\T\vec c_2 & \vec c_1\T\vec c_3 \\ \vec c_2\T\vec c_1 & \vec c_2\T\vec c_2 & \vec c_2\T\vec c_3 \\ \vec c_3\T\vec c_1 & \vec c_3\T\vec c_2 & \vec c_3\T\vec c_3 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Since $\vec c_i\T\vec c_j = \vec c_j\T\vec c_i$, the matrix is symmetric and six dot products are enough. Multiply the five pairs of entries and add:

\[ \begin{aligned} \vec c_1\T\vec c_1 &= 400^2 + 100^2 + 0^2 + 100^2 + 400^2 \\ &= 160000 + 10000 + 0 + 10000 + 160000 = 340000, \\ \vec c_1\T\vec c_2 &= 400(-20) + 100(-10) + 0\cdot 0 + 100\cdot 10 + 400\cdot 20 \\ &= -8000 - 1000 + 0 + 1000 + 8000 = 0, \\ \vec c_1\T\vec c_3 &= 400 + 100 + 0 + 100 + 400 = 1000, \\ \vec c_2\T\vec c_2 &= (-20)^2 + (-10)^2 + 0^2 + 10^2 + 20^2 = 400 + 100 + 0 + 100 + 400 = 1000, \\ \vec c_2\T\vec c_3 &= -20 - 10 + 0 + 10 + 20 = 0, \\ \vec c_3\T\vec c_3 &= 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 5. \end{aligned} \]

So

\[ \mat X\T\mat X = \begin{bmatrix} 340000 & 0 & 1000 \\ 0 & 1000 & 0 \\ 1000 & 0 & 5 \end{bmatrix}. \]

The two zeros come from the symmetric positions: in $\vec c_1\T\vec c_2$ and $\vec c_2\T\vec c_3$ the terms for $x$ and $-x$ cancel in pairs.

Computing $\mat X\T\vec y$. Entry $i$ is row $i$ of $\mat X\T$ times $\vec y$, the dot product $\vec c_i\T\vec y$:

\[ \begin{aligned} \vec c_1\T\vec y &= 400\cdot 274 + 100\cdot 673 + 0\cdot 887 + 100\cdot 861 + 400\cdot 680 \\ &= 109600 + 67300 + 0 + 86100 + 272000 = 535000, \\ \vec c_2\T\vec y &= (-20)(274) + (-10)(673) + 0\cdot 887 + 10\cdot 861 + 20\cdot 680 \\ &= -5480 - 6730 + 0 + 8610 + 13600 = 10000, \\ \vec c_3\T\vec y &= 274 + 673 + 887 + 861 + 680 = 3375. \end{aligned} \]

The normal equations $\mat X\T\mat X\,(\ell, m, b) = \mat X\T\vec y$ are therefore

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 340000 & 0 & 1000 \\ 0 & 1000 & 0 \\ 1000 & 0 & 5 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \ell \\ m \\ b \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 535000 \\ 10000 \\ 3375 \end{bmatrix}. \]

(b) The middle normal equation stands alone: $1000\,m = 10000$, so $m = 10$. The other two are

\[ 340000\,\ell + 1000\,b = 535000, \qquad 1000\,\ell + 5\,b = 3375 . \]

Subtract 200 times the second from the first: $(340000 - 200000)\,\ell = 535000 - 675000$, so $140000\,\ell = -140000$ and $\ell = -1$. Then $5\,b = 3375 + 1000 = 4375$, so $b = 875$. The best parabola is

\[ y = -x^2 + 10x + 875 \qquad (y \text{ in mJ},\ x \text{ in cm}), \]

and the residual is $(-1, -2, 12, -14, 5)$ mJ: no entry is larger than 14 mJ in size.

(c) Expanding the energy equation, $y = -\tfrac{\kappa}{2}x^2 + \kappa x_0\,x + \tfrac{\kappa}{2}(A^2 - x_0^2)$, so $\ell = -\kappa/2$ and $m = \kappa x_0$. Hence

\[ \kappa = -2\ell = 2\ \text{mJ/cm}^2 = 20\ \text{N/m}, \qquad x_0 = \frac{m}{\kappa} = \frac{10}{2} = 5\ \text{cm}, \]

using $1\ \text{mJ/cm}^2 = (10^{-3}\ \text{J})/(10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2) = 10\ \text{N/m}$. The largest kinetic energy, at $x_0 = 5$ cm, is $-25 + 50 + 875 = 900\ \text{mJ} = \tfrac12\kappa A^2$, which gives $A^2 = 900\ \text{cm}^2$ and an amplitude $A = 30$ cm.

The three columns of $\mat X$ are independent because the positions take at least three different values, so $\mat X\T\mat X$ is invertible and the fit is unique.

Where this is going

The two ingredients we assembled today — a rectangular matrix $\mat X$ and the symmetric positive definite combination $\mat X\T\mat X$ — reappear on Monday wearing physics. In springs and masses the rectangular matrix will be a difference matrix $\mat A$, a diagonal matrix $\mat C$ of material constants will sit in the middle, and equilibrium will read $\mat A\T\mat C\mat A\,\vec u = \vec f$. Least squares is the case $\mat C = \eye$.
