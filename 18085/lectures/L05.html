Lecture 6

The Delta Function and Green's Functions

Monday, September 28, 2026 MW 9:30–11:00, 6-120 Reading: Strang §1.4

Download PDF

After this lecture you should be able to
  • Construct $\delta(x)$ as the limit of a family of ordinary functions, and say what it means for that limit to have unit area.
  • Use the sifting property to read $\delta(x-x_0)$ as an infinitely fine filter that samples a function at one point.
  • Identify the step, ramp, and quadratic spline as the successive antiderivatives of $\delta$.
  • Solve a differential equation with a point load, and assemble the solution for a general load from the Green's function by superposition.

1 · Physical intuition: a finite amount of stuff in no space at all

The delta function models point concentrations: a finite amount of “stuff” packed into an infinitesimal region of space or time. Point masses, point charges, instantaneous impulses — in each case the total (mass, charge, momentum transferred) is a perfectly good finite number, but the region carrying it has shrunk to nothing.

Nothing in ordinary calculus describes that. A function with a finite value at one point and zero elsewhere integrates to zero, not to one. So we build the object we want as a limit.

2 · Mathematical construction: a limit of a family of functions

Take a family of ordinary rectangle functions, each one narrower than the last, and each one scaled in height so that the area under it stays equal to $1$:

Now take the limit as the rectangle width goes to $0$. The height must run off to infinity to keep the area fixed, and what is left is an infinitely concentrated spike at the origin — the delta function $\delta(x)$:

Since every member of the family had area $1$, it is clear that the limit should too:

\[ \boxed{\ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \delta(x)\,dx = 1 \ } \]
Remark · The family is not unique

Rectangles are convenient but not special. We could equally take the limit of a different family of functions — for instance increasingly narrow Gaussians, which are smooth rather than piecewise constant. Every reasonable family with unit area and shrinking support gives the same object, because what survives the limit is not the shape but the one number the shape carries.

3 · The sifting property

Shift the spike to sit at $x_0$ instead of $0$, and multiply by a function $f$ before integrating. Away from $x_0$ the spike contributes nothing; at $x_0$ it contributes all of its unit area, weighted by the value of $f$ there. So

\[ \boxed{\ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,\delta(x-x_0)\,dx = f(x_0). \ } \]

This is the property to remember. It says $\delta(x-x_0)$ is an infinitely fine filter that samples a function at the single point $x_0$ and reports back that one value.

Key idea

$\delta$ is defined by what it does inside an integral, not by its values. Every computation with $\delta$ in this course is an application of $\int f(x)\delta(x-x_0)\,dx = f(x_0)$ and its special case $\int \delta = 1$.

Watch out · It is not a function

$\delta(x)$ is not really a “proper” function, and strictly speaking it makes rigorous sense only under an integral. Asking for the number $\delta(0)$ is not a well-posed question. In engineering and physics practice we nonetheless treat it as a real function, trusting that the mathematics can be made completely rigorous if necessary — and it can, in the theory of distributions. Just do not be surprised when a manipulation that never puts $\delta$ under an integral produces nonsense.

4 · The antiderivatives of $\delta$

Because $\delta$ models point concentrations, it turns up on the right-hand side of differential equations. Solving those equations means integrating $\delta$, so it pays to know its antiderivatives. Integrating once accumulates the whole unit of area the instant we pass the origin:

\[ \text{1st antiderivative:}\quad \int_{-\infty}^{x} \delta(x')\,dx' = \Theta(x) \qquad \text{the step function,} \]

and integrating again, and again,

\[ \text{2nd:}\quad \int_{-\infty}^{x} \Theta(x')\,dx' = r(x) \quad \text{the ramp,} \qquad \text{3rd:}\quad \int_{-\infty}^{x} r(x')\,dx' = S(x) \quad \text{a quadratic spline.} \]

Notice how the smoothness improves at each step: $\delta$ is not a function at all, $\Theta$ jumps, $r$ is continuous with a corner, and $S$ is continuously differentiable. Each integration buys one degree of regularity. That is worth remembering, because a point load in a differential equation produces a solution that is exactly this much smoother than the load.

5 · Delta functions as a pointwise basis

Here is a fancy way of re-writing an arbitrary function $f$:

\[ f(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(y)\,\delta(x-y)\,dy . \]

Read the right-hand side as a superposition: $f$ is a continuous “sum” of spikes, one at every point $y$, with the number $f(y)$ as the coefficient. In the language of Lecture 5, the shifted deltas $\{\delta(\,\cdot\,-y)\}$ act as a basis for functions, and the coefficients in that basis are just the values of $f$ itself.

That reformulation looks like it says nothing. It is in fact the whole method of this lecture, because we already know what linearity does to a superposition.

6 · Green's functions

Suppose we have a linear differential equation. Use as the running example

\[ -\frac{d^{2}u}{dx^{2}} = f(x), \qquad u(0) = 0, \quad u'(1) = 0 , \]

the fixed–free problem from Lecture 3, now on the continuous side.

Suppose we can solve this equation for the special case of a single point load at $y$:

\[ -\frac{d^{2} g(x,y)}{dx^{2}} = \delta(x-y), \qquad g(0,y) = 0, \quad \pd{g}{x}\Big|_{x=1} = 0 . \]

Note that the boundary conditions are the same ones; only the load has changed. Then the solution for a general right-hand side $f(x)$ is

\[ \boxed{\ u(x) = \int_{0}^{1} f(y)\, g(x,y)\, dy \ } \]

which follows from the principle of superposition, given that

\[ f(x) = \int_{0}^{1} f(y)\,\delta(x-y)\,dy . \]

The load is a superposition of point loads with weights $f(y)$; the operator is linear; so the response is the same superposition of the point-load responses $g(x,y)$.

The function $g(x,y)$ is known as the Green's function of the problem.

Key idea · Solve once, solve forever

Solve the equation once, for a unit point load at an arbitrary position $y$, and you have solved it for every load: integrate $g$ against $f$. The Green's function is the response of the system to a unit impulse, and superposition does the rest.

Remark · The discrete analogue is the inverse matrix

On a grid, the load $f$ becomes a vector and $-d^2/dx^2$ becomes a second-difference matrix $\mat A$. A point load at node $j$ becomes the unit vector $\vec e_j$, and the response is $\mat A\inv \vec e_j$ — column $j$ of $\mat A\inv$. So the Green's function is what the inverse matrix turns into when the grid spacing goes to zero, and $u = \int f g\,dy$ is what $\vec u = \mat A\inv \vec f$ turns into. Same statement, two sides of the discretisation.

7 · Worked example: a point load at $x=\tfrac13$ at the board

Let us actually carry this out. Solve

\[ -\frac{d^{2}u}{dx^{2}} = \delta\!\left(x - \tfrac13\right), \qquad u(0) = 0, \quad u'(1) = 0 . \]

Step 1: away from the load, the solution is a straight line

For $x \neq \tfrac13$ the right-hand side vanishes, so $u'' = 0$ and $u$ is linear on each side:

\[ u(x) = \begin{cases} Cx + D, & 0 \le x < \tfrac13,\\[2pt] Ex + F, & \tfrac13 < x \le 1.\end{cases} \]

Step 2: impose the boundary conditions

From $u(0) = 0$ we get $D = 0$. From $u'(1) = 0$ we get $E = 0$, so the solution is flat to the right of the load:

\[ u(x) = Cx \ \ \text{on the left}, \qquad u(x) = F \ \ \text{on the right}. \]

Step 3: the load fixes the jump in slope

Integrate the equation across the load, from $\tfrac13-\varepsilon$ to $\tfrac13+\varepsilon$, and use $\int \delta = 1$:

\[ -\int_{1/3-\varepsilon}^{1/3+\varepsilon} u''\,dx = \int_{1/3-\varepsilon}^{1/3+\varepsilon} \delta\!\left(x-\tfrac13\right) dx = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad u'\!\left(\tfrac13^-\right) - u'\!\left(\tfrac13^+\right) = 1 . \]

The slopes are $C$ on the left and $0$ on the right, so $C = 1$.

Step 4: $u$ itself must be continuous

A jump in $u$ would make $u''$ contain a derivative of a delta, which is not what the right-hand side says. Matching the two pieces at $x = \tfrac13$ gives $F = C\cdot\tfrac13 = \tfrac13$. Therefore

\[ \boxed{\ u(x) = \begin{cases} x, & 0 \le x \le \tfrac13,\\[3pt] \tfrac13, & \tfrac13 \le x \le 1.\end{cases}\ } \]

The solution is exactly the ramp function of §4, bent flat at the load: it rises with slope $1$ until it reaches the point source, loses all of its slope there, and coasts. Compactly, $u(x) = x - r\!\left(x-\tfrac13\right)$.

Key idea · What a point load looks like

$-u'' = \delta(x-y)$ says: the graph of $u$ is straight everywhere except at $x=y$, where it has a corner, and the size of the drop in slope across that corner is exactly the strength of the load. The delta did not survive into the answer; it survived as a kink.

The Green's function itself

Nothing above used the value $\tfrac13$. Repeating the four steps with the load at a general point $y \in (0,1)$ gives $C=1$, $F=y$, and hence

\[ g(x,y) = \begin{cases} x, & x \le y,\\ y, & x \ge y,\end{cases} \qquad\text{that is,}\qquad g(x,y) = \min(x,y). \]

So for any load $f$ on the fixed–free bar,

\[ u(x) = \int_0^1 f(y)\, \min(x,y)\, dy . \]
Remark · $g$ is symmetric

$\min(x,y) = \min(y,x)$: the displacement at $x$ due to a unit load at $y$ equals the displacement at $y$ due to a unit load at $x$. This is the continuous shadow of the fact that our second-difference matrices are symmetric, and their inverses with them. When we build $\mat A\T\Cmat\mat A$ in the springs-and-masses lecture that symmetry will come back as a statement about energy.

Where this is going

We solved this problem by breaking the load into point sources — the basis in which the load is trivial — and adding up the responses. Next time we look for the other natural basis: the one in which the operator is trivial. Eigenvectors are the directions a matrix does not mix, and expanding in them turns matrix multiplication into multiplication by a number, exactly as expanding in deltas turned a general load into a pile of spikes.
