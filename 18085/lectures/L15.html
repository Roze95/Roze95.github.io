Lecture 16

Time Stepping: Simpson's Rule and Runge–Kutta

Download PDF

After this lecture you should be able to
  • Derive Simpson's rule in two ways: by cancelling the $h^3$ errors of the midpoint rule and the trapezoid, and by integrating a parabola.
  • Find the error of Simpson's rule, $-\frac{h^5}{2880}f^{(4)}$ per step, and see why the rule has no error for cubics.
  • Build RK4 from Simpson's rule by predicting the missing values with Euler steps.
  • Show that RK4 is fourth order, first on the test equation $\dot y = \mu y$ and then for any $\dot y = f(y)$.

  • Compare the five methods of Lectures 15 and 16 on accuracy, stability and cost.

1 · What we take from Lecture 15

Every method of Lecture 15 starts from the integral form of $\dot{\vec y} = \vec f(t, \vec y)$ over one step,

\[ \vec y(t_{n+1}) = \vec y(t_n) + \int_{t_n}^{t_{n+1}} \vec f\bigl(t, \vec y(t)\bigr)\,dt, \]

and replaces the integral by an area. Along the true solution the integrand is a function of $t$ alone, $f(t) = \dot y(t)$, and we write dots for its time derivatives: $\dot f = \ddot y$, $\ddot f = \dddot y$. The area sketches of Lecture 15 showed what each rule misses in one step:

rulearea exact minus ruleused by
left rectangle$h\,f(t_n)$$+\frac{h^2}{2}\,\dot f$forward Euler
right rectangle$h\,f(t_{n+1})$$-\frac{h^2}{2}\,\dot f$backward Euler
midpoint$h\,f(t_{n+1/2})$$+\frac{h^3}{24}\,\ddot f$leapfrog
trapezoid$\frac{h}{2}\bigl(f(t_n) + f(t_{n+1})\bigr)$$-\frac{h^3}{12}\,\ddot f$trapezoidal

A plus sign means the rule gives too little, a minus sign too much. None of these is better than second order. Two tools from Lecture 15 take us further today: Taylor series about the middle of the step, where the odd powers cancel (as in leapfrog's centred differences), and predicting an unknown value with a forward Euler step. The first gives Simpson's rule, a fourth-order rule for the integral (Section 2). The second turns Simpson's rule into a time stepper, RK4 (Section 3).

2 · Simpson's rule

In this section $f = f(t)$ is a function of $t$ alone, and we write $f_n = f(t_n)$, $f_m = f(t_{n+1/2})$ and $f_{n+1} = f(t_{n+1})$ for its values at the start, the midpoint and the end of the step.

One expansion for every rule

Measure time from the midpoint, $s = t - t_{n+1/2}$, so that the step is $-h/2 \le s \le h/2$. Taylor's theorem about the midpoint, with every derivative taken at $t_{n+1/2}$, gives

\[ f(t_{n+1/2} + s) = f_m + s\,\dot f + \frac{s^2}{2}\,\ddot f + \frac{s^3}{6}\,\dddot f + \frac{s^4}{24}\,f^{(4)} + \cdots . \]

Integrate term by term over $-h/2 \le s \le h/2$. The odd powers $s$ and $s^3$ integrate to zero over the symmetric interval, and for even $k$

\[ \int_{-h/2}^{h/2} s^k\,ds = 2\int_0^{h/2} s^k\,ds = \frac{2}{k+1}\Bigl(\frac{h}{2}\Bigr)^{k+1}, \qquad \text{so} \qquad \int_{-h/2}^{h/2} s^2\,ds = \frac{h^3}{12}, \qquad \int_{-h/2}^{h/2} s^4\,ds = \frac{h^5}{80}. \]

The exact integral over one step is therefore

\[ I = \int_{t_n}^{t_{n+1}} f(t)\,dt = h f_m + \frac12\cdot\frac{h^3}{12}\,\ddot f + \frac1{24}\cdot\frac{h^5}{80}\,f^{(4)} + \cdots = h f_m + \frac{h^3}{24}\,\ddot f + \frac{h^5}{1920}\,f^{(4)} + \cdots . \]

The midpoint rule and the trapezoid

The midpoint rule $M = h f_m$ is the first term, so

\[ I - M = \frac{h^3}{24}\,\ddot f + \frac{h^5}{1920}\,f^{(4)} + \cdots . \]

For the trapezoid $T = \frac{h}{2}(f_n + f_{n+1})$ we need the end values. They are the expansion at $s = -h/2$ and $s = +h/2$:

\[ \begin{aligned} f_n &= f_m - \frac{h}{2}\,\dot f + \frac{h^2}{8}\,\ddot f - \frac{h^3}{48}\,\dddot f + \frac{h^4}{384}\,f^{(4)} - \cdots, \\ f_{n+1} &= f_m + \frac{h}{2}\,\dot f + \frac{h^2}{8}\,\ddot f + \frac{h^3}{48}\,\dddot f + \frac{h^4}{384}\,f^{(4)} + \cdots . \end{aligned} \]

Add them: the odd powers cancel, as in leapfrog's centred second difference, and the even powers double,

\[ f_n + f_{n+1} = 2f_m + \frac{h^2}{4}\,\ddot f + \frac{h^4}{192}\,f^{(4)} + \cdots, \qquad T = \frac{h}{2}\,(f_n + f_{n+1}) = h f_m + \frac{h^3}{8}\,\ddot f + \frac{h^5}{384}\,f^{(4)} + \cdots . \]

Subtract from $I$:

\[ I - T = \Bigl(\frac1{24} - \frac18\Bigr)h^3\,\ddot f + \Bigl(\frac1{1920} - \frac1{384}\Bigr)h^5 f^{(4)} + \cdots = -\frac{h^3}{12}\,\ddot f - \frac{h^5}{480}\,f^{(4)} + \cdots . \]

These are the last two rows of the table in Section 1, now with their next terms.

Cancelling the $h^3$ terms

The two $h^3$ errors have opposite signs and the trapezoid's is twice as large: $\frac1{24}$ against $-\frac1{12}$. So take the midpoint rule twice and the trapezoid once,

\[ S = \frac{2M + T}{3}. \]

The weights add to 1, so $S$ is still a rule for the integral, and its error is the same combination of the two errors:

\[ \begin{aligned} I - S &= \frac{2\,(I - M) + (I - T)}{3} = \frac13\Bigl[\Bigl(\frac{2}{24} - \frac1{12}\Bigr)h^3\,\ddot f + \Bigl(\frac{2}{1920} - \frac1{480}\Bigr)h^5 f^{(4)}\Bigr] + \cdots \\ &= \frac13\Bigl[0 - \frac{2}{1920}\,h^5 f^{(4)}\Bigr] + \cdots = -\frac{h^5}{2880}\,f^{(4)} + \cdots . \end{aligned} \]

The $h^3$ term is gone. Written with the three values of $f$,

\[ S = \frac13\Bigl(2hf_m + \frac{h}{2}\,(f_n + f_{n+1})\Bigr) = \frac{h}{6}\,(f_n + 4f_m + f_{n+1}). \]

Only even derivatives of $f$ appear in these errors, because the odd powers of $s$ integrate to zero. So the third derivative never enters, and $S$ has no error at all when $f$ is a cubic.

Simpson's rule \[ \int_{t_n}^{t_{n+1}} f(t)\,dt \approx \frac{h}{6}\,(f_n + 4f_m + f_{n+1}), \qquad \text{error } -\frac{h^5}{2880}\,f^{(4)} \text{ per step.} \]

It is the combination of the midpoint rule and the trapezoid in which their $h^3$ errors cancel.

The same weights from a parabola

A second route gives the same rule. Replace $f$ by the parabola through the three points $(t_n, f_n)$, $(t_{n+1/2}, f_m)$ and $(t_{n+1}, f_{n+1})$, and integrate the parabola. With $s$ as before, write the parabola as $p(s) = a + bs + cs^2$. The three conditions are

\[ p(0) = a = f_m, \qquad p\bigl(-\tfrac{h}{2}\bigr) = a - \frac{bh}{2} + \frac{ch^2}{4} = f_n, \qquad p\bigl(\tfrac{h}{2}\bigr) = a + \frac{bh}{2} + \frac{ch^2}{4} = f_{n+1}. \]

Adding the last two gives $f_n + f_{n+1} = 2a + ch^2/2$, so $c = 2\,(f_n + f_{n+1} - 2f_m)/h^2$. Now integrate. The odd term $bs$ integrates to zero over the symmetric interval, and $\int_{-h/2}^{h/2} s^2\,ds = h^3/12$:

\[ \int_{-h/2}^{h/2} p(s)\,ds = a\,h + c\,\frac{h^3}{12} = hf_m + \frac{h}{6}\,(f_n + f_{n+1} - 2f_m) = \frac{h}{6}\,(f_n + 4f_m + f_{n+1}). \]

The weights are $\frac16, \frac46, \frac16$. The rule integrates cubics without error as well: for $f = s^3$ the integral and the rule are both zero. The first function it gets wrong is $f = s^4$, whose integral is $\int_{-h/2}^{h/2} s^4\,ds = h^5/80$ while the rule gives $\frac{h}{6}\bigl(\frac{h^4}{16} + 0 + \frac{h^4}{16}\bigr) = h^5/48$. The error (exact minus rule) is $h^5/80 - h^5/48 = -h^5/120$, and since $f^{(4)} = 24$ for this $f$, this agrees with the error $-\frac{h^5}{2880}f^{(4)}$ found by cancelling.

Example 2.1 (Three rules on one integral). Given. $\int_0^{0.5} e^t\,dt = e^{0.5} - 1 = 0.6487213$, taken as one step with $h = 0.5$.

Answer. Every derivative of $e^t$ is $e^t$, which is $e^{0.25} = 1.2840254$ at the midpoint. The three rules give

and $S$ is also $\frac{0.5}{6}\bigl(1 + 4e^{0.25} + e^{0.5}\bigr)$. Against the exact value:

Many steps

Over an interval of length $L$ split into $N$ steps of $h = L/N$, the step errors $-\frac{h^5}{2880}f^{(4)}$ add up to about $-\frac{h^4}{2880}\int f^{(4)}\,dt$: Simpson's rule is fourth order, and halving $h$ divides the error by about 16. For $\int_0^1 e^t\,dt = e - 1$:

If $\dot y = f(t)$ depends on $t$ alone, the step $y_{n+1} = y_n + \frac{h}{6}(f_n + 4f_m + f_{n+1})$ is a fourth-order time stepper. When $f$ depends on $y$ as well, the values $f_m$ and $f_{n+1}$ are not available, and that is the next section.

3 · Runge–Kutta: the idea

Every method so far is a way to estimate one area. Over a step of size $h$,

\[ \vec Y_{n+1} = \vec Y_n + \int_{t_n}^{t_{n+1}} \vec f\,dt, \]

so the step is decided by the height of $\vec f$ across the interval. Forward Euler looks at the slope once, at the start, and commits to it for the whole step. If the slope is changing, that one look is out of date before the step ends.

Runge–Kutta methods take several looks at the slope inside the step, then move once with a weighted average of what they saw. Nothing is solved, and earlier steps are not needed. The picture below shows the area each choice covers for $\dot y = y$, $y(0) = 1$, $h = 0.5$, drawn to scale. The true value is $e^{0.5} = 1.6487213$.

With one look we get a single rectangle, which misses the growth of the curve. With a look at the middle the rectangle sits at about the right average height. With four looks the area becomes three strips of widths $h/6$, $4h/6$ and $h/6$: these are the weights $1, 4, 1$ of Simpson's rule, and the strips cover the area almost exactly.

The four looks

Simpson's rule wants the height at the start, the middle and the end. The middle and the end lie in the future, so we do not know $\vec Y$ there. RK4 scouts ahead, each time from $\vec Y_n$, using the latest slope it has:

Look 1:slope where we stand $\vec k_1 = \vec f(t_n,\ \vec Y_n)$
Look 2:walk half a step with $\vec k_1$, look again $\vec k_2 = \vec f\bigl(t_n + \frac{h}{2},\ \vec Y_n + \frac{h}{2}\vec k_1\bigr)$
Look 3:walk half a step with $\vec k_2$ instead, look again $\vec k_3 = \vec f\bigl(t_n + \frac{h}{2},\ \vec Y_n + \frac{h}{2}\vec k_2\bigr)$
Look 4:walk a full step with $\vec k_3$, look at the end $\vec k_4 = \vec f\bigl(t_n + h,\ \vec Y_n + h\vec k_3\bigr)$
The RK4 step \[ \vec Y_{n+1} = \vec Y_n + \frac{h}{6}\,\bigl(\vec k_1 + 2\vec k_2 + 2\vec k_3 + \vec k_4\bigr) \]

These are Simpson's weights $1, 4, 1$, with the 4 shared between the two looks at the middle. Each step costs four evaluations of $\vec f$. The error over a fixed time is of order $h^4$: halve $h$ and it falls by about 16.

The derivation, with the proof that the method is fourth order, is in the Supplementary Information at the end of these notes (Section S).

4 · Comparing the five methods

With RK4 there are five methods: forward Euler, backward Euler, leapfrog and the trapezoidal method from Lecture 15, and RK4. This section compares them on the oscillator $\ddot u + u = 0$ and on a fast decay.

4.1 · RK4 on the oscillator

For $\ddot u + u = 0$ the growth matrix of Section S is $\mat G_{RK} = \eye + h\mat A + \frac12(h\mat A)^2 + \frac16(h\mat A)^3 + \frac1{24}(h\mat A)^4$. Since $\mat A^2 = -\eye$, the powers of $h\mat A$ are $(h\mat A)^2 = -h^2\eye$, $(h\mat A)^3 = -h^3\mat A$ and $(h\mat A)^4 = h^4\eye$. Collecting the multiples of $\eye$ and of $\mat A$,

\[ \mat G_{RK} = \Bigl(1 - \frac{h^2}{2} + \frac{h^4}{24}\Bigr)\eye + \Bigl(h - \frac{h^3}{6}\Bigr)\mat A = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ -b & a \end{bmatrix}, \qquad a = 1 - \frac{h^2}{2} + \frac{h^4}{24}, \quad b = h - \frac{h^3}{6}. \]

This is a multiple of a rotation, with eigenvalues $a \pm ib$. Expanding the squares,

\[ a^2 = 1 - h^2 + \frac{h^4}{3} - \frac{h^6}{24} + \frac{h^8}{576}, \qquad b^2 = h^2 - \frac{h^4}{3} + \frac{h^6}{36}, \]

so the terms in $h^2$ and $h^4$ cancel and

\[ \abs{\lambda}^2 = a^2 + b^2 = 1 - \frac{h^6}{72} + \frac{h^8}{576}. \]

Then $\abs{\lambda} \le 1$ if and only if $h^8/576 \le h^6/72$, that is $h^2 \le 8$. So $\abs{\lambda} < 1$ for $0 < h < 2\sqrt2 \approx 2.83$, and the method blows up for $h > 2\sqrt2$. For small $h$ the damping is slight, about $1 - h^6/144$ per step ($0.99989$ at $h = 0.5$). For the angle, $a + ib$ is the series $1 + ih + (ih)^2/2 + (ih)^3/6 + (ih)^4/24$, which differs from the exponential by $(ih)^5/120$:

\[ a + ib = e^{ih} - \frac{(ih)^5}{120} + \cdots = e^{ih}\Bigl(1 - \frac{ih^5}{120} + \cdots\Bigr), \qquad \text{so} \qquad \theta = \arctan\frac{b}{a} = h - \frac{h^5}{120} + \cdots . \]

The computed oscillation falls behind by about $h^5/120$ per step.

Example 4.1 (One RK4 step by hand). Given. $\vec f(t, \vec y) = \mat A\vec y = (v, -u)$, $\vec Y_0 = (1, 0)$ and $h = 0.5$.

Find. What are the four slopes $\vec k_1, \ldots, \vec k_4$ and $\vec Y_1$? How close is $\vec Y_1$ to the true point $(\cos 0.5, -\sin 0.5)$?

Answer. With $h$ as a letter the answer is the first column of $\mat G_{RK}$, $\vec Y_1 = (a, -b)$ with $a = 1 - h^2/2 + h^4/24$ and $b = h - h^3/6$. Step by step at $h = 0.5$, each slope is $\vec f$ at a trial point:

\[ \begin{aligned} &\vec k_1 = \mat A\vec Y_0 = (0, -1), && \\ &\vec Y_0 + 0.25\,\vec k_1 = (1, -0.25), && \vec k_2 = (-0.25, -1), \\ &\vec Y_0 + 0.25\,\vec k_2 = (0.9375, -0.25), && \vec k_3 = (-0.25, -0.9375), \\ &\vec Y_0 + 0.5\,\vec k_3 = (0.875, -0.46875), && \vec k_4 = (-0.46875, -0.875). \end{aligned} \]

Then $\vec k_1 + 2\vec k_2 + 2\vec k_3 + \vec k_4 = (-1.46875, -5.75)$ and

\[ \vec Y_1 = (1, 0) + \tfrac1{12}\,(-1.46875, -5.75) = (0.877604, -0.479167). \]

The same point comes from $\mat G_{RK}$ with $a = 0.877604$ and $b = 0.479167$. The true point is $(0.877583, -0.479426)$, so after one step RK4 is off by $0.00026$. After one step of the same size forward Euler is off by $0.124$ and the trapezoidal method by $0.010$.

4.3 · One eigenvalue at a time

For the table below and for the stability regions, look at one eigenvalue at a time, with the test equation of Section S. For the scalar equation $\dot y = \mu y$ each method multiplies $Y_n$ by a factor $R(z)$, with $z = h\mu$, against $e^z$ for the true solution: forward Euler gives $Y_{n+1} = (1 + h\mu)\,Y_n$, so $R(z) = 1 + z$, and in the same way

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{backward Euler:} \quad & R(z) = \frac{1}{1 - z}, \\ \text{trapezoidal:} \quad & R(z) = \frac{1 + z/2}{1 - z/2}, \\ \text{RK4:} \quad & R(z) = 1 + z + \frac{z^2}{2} + \frac{z^3}{6} + \frac{z^4}{24}. \end{aligned} \]

For a system $\dot{\vec y} = \mat A\vec y$ these methods have $\mat G = R(h\mat A)$, so the eigenvalues of $\mat G$ are the numbers $R(h\mu)$, one for each eigenvalue $\mu$ of $\mat A$ (the letter $\lambda$ stays with the eigenvalues of $\mat G$). Leapfrog is built for second-order equations and has no single factor $R(z)$. The oscillator has $\mu = \pm i$, so $z = \pm ih$ lies on the imaginary axis; a mode that decays like $e^{-at}$ has $z = -ah$ on the negative real axis. The stability region of a method is the set of $z$ with $\abs{R(z)} \le 1$.

For forward Euler it is the disc $\abs{1 + z} \le 1$, of radius 1 about $z = -1$; the imaginary axis touches it only at $z = 0$, so forward Euler is unstable for the oscillator at every $h > 0$. For backward Euler it is $\abs{1 - z} \ge 1$, everything outside the disc of radius 1 about $z = +1$. That contains the whole left half-plane, so every decaying mode stays bounded at every step size; methods with this property are called A-stable. For the trapezoidal method $\abs{R(z)} \le 1$ means $\abs{1 + z/2} \le \abs{1 - z/2}$, that is $\abs{z + 2} \le \abs{z - 2}$: $z$ is no farther from $-2$ than from $+2$, which holds if and only if $\operatorname{Re} z \le 0$. So its stability region is the left half-plane, and its edge, the imaginary axis, is where the oscillator's $z = \pm ih$ lie.

4.5 · Accuracy for the work

How many steps does each method need? Ask for the computed point $(U, V)$ to be within 1% of the radius of the true point at the end of ten periods (for leapfrog, $V$ is compared with the velocity half a step later). The fewest steps per period that do it are:

The first-order methods need about twenty thousand steps per period, the second-order methods about a hundred, RK4 seventeen. The orders of accuracy show as slopes on a log-log plot of the error against the number of steps: doubling the steps halves the Euler errors, quarters the leapfrog and trapezoidal errors, and divides the RK4 error by 16. At the same step leapfrog's error is half that of the trapezoidal method, because its phase error per step is $h^3/24$ against $h^3/12$.

4.6 · Stability regions

The stability regions $\abs{R(z)} \le 1$ of Section 4.3 are shaded below for four of the methods, with the oscillator's $z = \pm0.5i$ (dots, $h = 0.5$) and the fast decay $z = -5$ of Example 4.2 (cross). Leapfrog has no region of this kind: for $\ddot u = -\omega^2 u$ its condition is the stretch $\abs{\omega h} < 2$ of the imaginary axis. (Some books give the name leapfrog to the two-step method $Y_{n+1} = Y_{n-1} + 2h\,f(Y_n)$ for a first-order equation; that method is stable only on the stretch $\abs{z} < 1$ of the imaginary axis. It is not the staggered method of this lecture.)

The oscillator's dots lie outside the forward Euler disc, inside the backward Euler region (where they are damped), on the edge of the trapezoidal half-plane, and just inside the RK4 region ($\abs{R(\pm0.5i)} = 0.99989$). The RK4 region reaches $\pm2\sqrt2\,i$ on the imaginary axis and $-2.785$ on the real axis. Only the implicit methods contain the cross.

Example 4.2 (A fast decay). Given. An RC circuit with time constant $RC = 20$ ms, so that its capacitor voltage $x$ obeys $\dot x = -x/(RC) = -50\,x$, is one part of a larger simulation whose slow parts are stepped with $h = 0.1$ s.

Find. What does each method multiply the voltage $x$ by at each step? Which methods keep it bounded?

Answer. Here $z = -50\times0.1 = -5$, and the true factor per step is $e^{-5} = 0.0067$.

Leapfrog does not apply to this first-order equation. Backward Euler gives the right picture: the fast part is gone after a step or two. The trapezoidal method keeps it bounded but flips its sign at every step. Forward Euler and RK4 blow up; they would need $h \le 2/50 = 0.04$ s and $h \le 2.785/50 = 0.056$ s, steps set by a part of the circuit that does nothing of interest after its first hundred milliseconds or so (a few time constants). Problems like this, with fast modes that decay and slow modes that we want to follow, are called stiff, and implicit methods are made for them.

Which method when
  • Long runs of oscillations, where the energy must not drift (orbits, molecular dynamics, waves): leapfrog, which is explicit and cheap, inside its step limit; or the trapezoidal method, which keeps the energy of a linear oscillator at any step, at the cost of a solve.
  • Smooth problems without fast decaying modes, where accuracy for the work matters: RK4, or an adaptive Runge–Kutta pair such as ode45.
  • Stiff problems: backward Euler, or the backward differentiation formulas that extend it to higher order, which damp the fast modes at any step.
  • Forward Euler: for understanding, and as a building block; rarely on its own.

Answer to Exercise 4.3

(a) $\omega = 1/\sqrt{10^{-3}\cdot10^{-5}} = 10^4$ rad/s, so $f = 1.59$ kHz and $T = 2\pi/\omega = 628\ \mu$s.

(b) In the time $\omega t$ the equation is our oscillator, with the step $\omega h$. The trapezoidal method is stable at every step, and so is backward Euler, which damps the oscillation. Forward Euler is unstable at every step.

(c) The step is $h = T/N$ with $N$ from the table: forward Euler $0.032\ \mu$s, backward Euler $0.032\ \mu$s, leapfrog $6.2\ \mu$s, trapezoidal $4.4\ \mu$s, RK4 $37\ \mu$s (with 68 evaluations per period). These steps are far below the stability limits of (b): here accuracy, not stability, sets the step.

Appendix: Simpson's rule, RK4 and the five methods in Python

The script below repeats Examples 2.1 and S.1 and the table of Simpson's rule with $N$ steps, builds the five growth matrices, repeats the two-step comparison and Example 4.1, finds the steps per period for 1% by bisection, and prints the factors of Example 4.2. It needs only NumPy.

"""Lecture 16: Simpson's rule, RK4, and five time-stepping methods for u'' + u = 0 (y' = Ay, y = (u, v))."""
import numpy as np

# 1. three rules for the integral of e^t over one step [0, 0.5]
h = 0.5
M = h * np.exp(h / 2)                                        # midpoint
T = h / 2 * (1 + np.exp(h))                                  # trapezoid
S = (2 * M + T) / 3                                          # Simpson = (h/6)(f_n + 4 f_m + f_n+1)
exact = np.exp(h) - 1
print(f"integral of e^t on [0, 0.5]: exact {exact:.7f}")
for name, r in (("midpoint", M), ("trapezoid", T), ("Simpson", S)):
    print(f"  {name:9s} {r:.7f}  exact minus rule {exact - r:+.7f}")

# 2. Simpson's rule with N steps on [0, 1]
for N in (1, 2, 4, 8):
    hN = 1 / N
    t = hN * np.arange(N)
    SN = np.sum(hN / 6 * (np.exp(t) + 4 * np.exp(t + hN / 2) + np.exp(t + hN)))
    print(f"  Simpson, N = {N}: exact minus rule {np.e - 1 - SN:.3e}")

A = np.array([[0.0, 1.0], [-1.0, 0.0]])
I = np.eye(2)
methods = ["forward Euler", "backward Euler", "leapfrog", "trapezoidal", "RK4"]

def growth_matrix(method, h):
    if method == "forward Euler":
        return I + h * A
    if method == "backward Euler":
        return np.linalg.inv(I - h * A)    # a real code solves with I - hA instead
    if method == "leapfrog":
        return np.array([[1.0, h], [-h, 1.0 - h * h]])
    if method == "trapezoidal":
        return np.linalg.solve(I - h * A / 2, I + h * A / 2)
    if method == "RK4":
        hA = h * A
        return I + hA + hA @ hA / 2 + hA @ hA @ hA / 6 + hA @ hA @ hA @ hA / 24

def rk4_step(f, t, y, h):                          # one RK4 step for any y' = f(t, y)
    k1 = f(t, y)
    k2 = f(t + h / 2, y + h / 2 * k1)
    k3 = f(t + h / 2, y + h / 2 * k2)
    k4 = f(t + h, y + h * k3)
    return y + h / 6 * (k1 + 2 * k2 + 2 * k3 + k4)

def start(method, h):                   # u(0) = 1, v(0) = 0; leapfrog gets half a kick
    return np.array([1.0, -h / 2 if method == "leapfrog" else 0.0])

def distance(method, y, t, h):    # leapfrog's V is the velocity half a step later
    s = h / 2 if method == "leapfrog" else 0.0
    return np.hypot(y[0] - np.cos(t), y[1] + np.sin(t + s))

# 1. two steps of h = 0.5, and the size and angle of the eigenvalues of G
h = 0.5
print("two steps of h = 0.5 from u = 1, v = 0:")
for m in methods:
    G = growth_matrix(m, h)
    y = G @ G @ start(m, h)
    lam = np.linalg.eigvals(G)[0]
    print(f"  {m:14s} U2 {y[0]:8.5f}  V2 {y[1]:8.5f}  off {distance(m, y, 1.0, h):.5f}"
          f"  |lambda| {abs(lam):.5f}  angle {abs(np.angle(lam)):.5f}")
y1 = rk4_step(lambda t, y: A @ y, 0.0, np.array([1.0, 0.0]), h)
print("  one RK4 step with stages:", np.round(y1, 6),
      " same as G:", np.allclose(y1, growth_matrix("RK4", h) @ [1.0, 0.0]))
print("RK4 for y' = y, one step of h = 0.5:", rk4_step(lambda t, y: y, 0.0, 1.0, 0.5),
      " exact", round(np.exp(0.5), 7))


# 3. fewest steps per period that keep the point within 1% after ten periods
def error_after(method, N, periods=10):
    h = 2 * np.pi / N
    n = N * periods
    y = np.linalg.matrix_power(growth_matrix(method, h), n) @ start(method, h)
    return distance(method, y, h * n, h)

print("steps per period for 1% after ten periods:")
for m in methods:
    lo, hi = 2, 10 ** 6
    while hi - lo > 1:                  # bisection: the error falls as N grows
        mid = (lo + hi) // 2
        lo, hi = (lo, mid) if error_after(m, mid) <= 0.01 else (mid, hi)
    work = 4 * hi if m == "RK4" else hi
    print(f"  {m:14s} {hi:6d} steps, {work:6d} evaluations or solves per period")

# 4. a fast decay y' = -50 y with h = 0.1: z = -5
z = -5.0
R = {"forward Euler": 1 + z, "backward Euler": 1 / (1 - z),
     "trapezoidal": (1 + z / 2) / (1 - z / 2),
     "RK4": 1 + z + z ** 2 / 2 + z ** 3 / 6 + z ** 4 / 24}
print("factor per step at z = -5, exact", round(np.exp(z), 4))
for m, r in R.items():
    print(f"  {m:14s} {r:8.4f}")

S · Supplementary Information: the derivation of RK4

This section derives the RK4 step summarised in Section 3: how the method follows from Simpson's rule, and why it is fourth order.

The missing values

For $\dot{\vec y} = \vec f(t, \vec y)$ Simpson's rule needs $\vec f$ at the midpoint and at the end of the step, and these need $\vec y(t_{n+1/2})$ and $\vec y(t_{n+1})$, which are not known yet. Lecture 15 had two answers to this kind of problem. Backward Euler and the trapezoidal method solve for the new value: they are implicit and cost a solve per step. Forward Euler uses only values it already has: it is explicit. Runge–Kutta methods stay explicit. They predict the missing values with Euler steps and use the predictions only inside $\vec f$.

Two stages: the midpoint method

The smallest example is the midpoint rule. Predict the midpoint with half a forward Euler step, take the slope there, and use it for the whole step:

\[ \vec k_1 = \vec f(t_n, \vec Y_n), \qquad \vec k_2 = \vec f\bigl(t_n + \tfrac{h}{2},\ \vec Y_n + \tfrac{h}{2}\,\vec k_1\bigr), \qquad \vec Y_{n+1} = \vec Y_n + h\,\vec k_2 . \]

How much does the prediction cost? Half a forward Euler step from $\vec y(t_n)$ misses $\frac12\bigl(\frac{h}{2}\bigr)^2\ddot{\vec y} = \frac{h^2}{8}\ddot{\vec y}$ (Lecture 15), so $\vec k_2$ is off the true midpoint slope by a multiple of $h^2$, and $h\,\vec k_2$ by a multiple of $h^3$. That is the same order as the error $\frac{h^3}{24}\ddot f$ of the midpoint rule itself, so one step is in error by $\Oh{h^3}$ and the method is second order.

To see the order in one line, use the test equation $\dot y = \mu y$, where $\mu$ is a number, possibly complex (for the oscillator $\mu = \pm i$, the eigenvalues of $\mat A$). Its exact step multiplies $y$ by

\[ e^{h\mu} = e^z = 1 + z + \frac{z^2}{2} + \frac{z^3}{6} + \frac{z^4}{24} + \frac{z^5}{120} + \cdots, \qquad z = h\mu, \]

and each method multiplies $Y_n$ by a factor $R(z)$ instead. For the midpoint method $k_1 = \mu Y_n$, the predicted midpoint is $Y_n + \frac{h}{2}k_1 = (1 + \frac{z}{2})Y_n$, so $k_2 = \mu(1 + \frac{z}{2})Y_n$ and

\[ Y_{n+1} = Y_n + h\,k_2 = \Bigl(1 + z + \frac{z^2}{2}\Bigr)Y_n, \qquad R(z) = 1 + z + \frac{z^2}{2}. \]

This matches $e^z$ up to $z^2$ and misses $\frac{z^3}{6}$: an error of order $h^3$ per step, second order.

Four stages

RK4 does the same for Simpson's rule, which needs the midpoint and the end. It predicts the midpoint twice and the end once:

$\vec k_1 = \vec f(t_n, \vec Y_n)$slope at the start,
$\vec k_2 = \vec f\bigl(t_n + \frac{h}{2},\ \vec Y_n + \frac{h}{2}\,\vec k_1\bigr)$ slope at a midpoint predicted with $\vec k_1$,
$\vec k_3 = \vec f\bigl(t_n + \frac{h}{2},\ \vec Y_n + \frac{h}{2}\,\vec k_2\bigr)$ slope at a midpoint predicted with $\vec k_2$,
$\vec k_4 = \vec f\bigl(t_n + h,\ \vec Y_n + h\,\vec k_3\bigr)$ slope at an end point predicted with $\vec k_3$.

Then it applies Simpson's rule with the midpoint slope taken as the average of $\vec k_2$ and $\vec k_3$:

\[ \vec Y_{n+1} = \vec Y_n + \frac{h}{6}\Bigl(\vec k_1 + 4\cdot\frac{\vec k_2 + \vec k_3}{2} + \vec k_4\Bigr) = \vec Y_n + \frac{h}{6}\bigl(\vec k_1 + 2\vec k_2 + 2\vec k_3 + \vec k_4\bigr). \]

The method is explicit: four evaluations of $\vec f$ per step and no solves. If $\vec f$ depends on $t$ only, then $\vec k_2 = \vec k_3$ and the update is Simpson's rule itself. Why two midpoint slopes rather than one? The test equation answers this.

The check on the test equation

For $\dot y = \mu y$ every trial point is $Y_n$ times a polynomial in $z$, and every slope is $\mu$ times the trial point:

\[ \begin{aligned} &k_1 = \mu Y_n, && \\ &Y_n + \tfrac{h}{2}k_1 = \bigl(1 + \tfrac{z}{2}\bigr)Y_n, && k_2 = \mu\bigl(1 + \tfrac{z}{2}\bigr)Y_n, \\ &Y_n + \tfrac{h}{2}k_2 = \bigl(1 + \tfrac{z}{2} + \tfrac{z^2}{4}\bigr)Y_n, && k_3 = \mu\bigl(1 + \tfrac{z}{2} + \tfrac{z^2}{4}\bigr)Y_n, \\ &Y_n + hk_3 = \bigl(1 + z + \tfrac{z^2}{2} + \tfrac{z^3}{4}\bigr)Y_n, && k_4 = \mu\bigl(1 + z + \tfrac{z^2}{2} + \tfrac{z^3}{4}\bigr)Y_n. \end{aligned} \]

Collect the coefficients of $\mu Y_n$ in $k_1 + 2k_2 + 2k_3 + k_4$:

$1$$z$ $z^2$$z^3$
$k_1$$1$$0$$0$$0$
$2k_2$$2$$1$$0$$0$
$2k_3$$2$$1$$\frac12$$0$
$k_4$$1$$1$$\frac12$$\frac14$
sum$6$$3$$1$$\frac14$

So $k_1 + 2k_2 + 2k_3 + k_4 = \mu\bigl(6 + 3z + z^2 + \frac{z^3}{4}\bigr)Y_n$, and with $\frac{h}{6}\,\mu = \frac{z}{6}$,

\[ Y_{n+1} = Y_n + \frac{z}{6}\Bigl(6 + 3z + z^2 + \frac{z^3}{4}\Bigr)Y_n = \Bigl(1 + z + \frac{z^2}{2} + \frac{z^3}{6} + \frac{z^4}{24}\Bigr)Y_n . \]

The factor $R(z) = 1 + z + \frac{z^2}{2} + \frac{z^3}{6} + \frac{z^4}{24}$ is $e^z$ up to $z^4$ and misses $\frac{z^5}{120}$: one step is in error by $\Oh{h^5}$, the error at a fixed time is $\Oh{h^4}$, and halving $h$ divides it by about 16.

The second midpoint slope is what makes the $z^3$ term right. With $4k_2$ in place of $2k_2 + 2k_3$ the sum would be $6 + 3z + \frac{z^2}{2} + \frac{z^3}{4}$, and $R(z) = 1 + z + \frac{z^2}{2} + \frac{z^3}{12} + \frac{z^4}{24}$, with $\frac1{12}$ where $e^z$ has $\frac16$: only second order.

For a system $\dot{\vec y} = \mat A\vec y$ the same algebra holds with $\mu$ replaced by $\mat A$, since powers of $\mat A$ commute. The growth matrix is the same polynomial,

\[ \mat G_{RK} = \eye + h\mat A + \frac{(h\mat A)^2}{2} + \frac{(h\mat A)^3}{6} + \frac{(h\mat A)^4}{24}, \]

the Taylor series of $e^{h\mat A}$ up to the fourth power.

Fourth order for any $f$: the Taylor check

We now check, with every step written out, that one RK4 step agrees with the Taylor series of the true solution up to the $h^4$ term. To keep the bookkeeping short take a single equation that does not depend on $t$ explicitly, $\dot y = f(y)$, start from $Y_n = y(t_n)$, and write $f$, $f'$, $f''$, $f'''$ for $f$ and its derivatives with respect to $y$, all evaluated at $Y_n$.

The true solution. Differentiate $\dot y = f(y)$ with the chain rule, using $\dot y = f$ each time:

\[ \begin{aligned} \ddot y &= f'\,\dot y = ff', \\ \dddot y &= \frac{d}{dt}(ff') = (f'f' + ff'')\dot y = ff'^2 + f^2f'', \\ y^{(4)} &= \frac{d}{dt}(ff'^2 + f^2f'') = (f'^3 + 2ff'f'' + 2ff'f'' + f^2f''')\dot y = ff'^3 + 4f^2f'f'' + f^3f''' . \end{aligned} \]

So the Taylor series of the true solution over one step is

\[ y(t_{n+1}) = Y_n + hf + \frac{h^2}{2}\,ff' + \frac{h^3}{6}\,(ff'^2 + f^2f'') + \frac{h^4}{24}\,(ff'^3 + 4f^2f'f'' + f^3f''') + \Oh{h^5}. \]

The stages. Each stage is $f$ at a shifted point, $f(Y_n + \delta)$, and Taylor's theorem in $\delta$ gives

\[ f(Y_n + \delta) = f + \delta f' + \frac{\delta^2}{2}\,f'' + \frac{\delta^3}{6}\,f''' + \cdots . \]

We keep every term up to $h^3$ in each stage (multiplied by $h$ in the update, that is $h^4$).

  • $k_1 = f$.

  • $k_2$: here $\delta = \frac{h}{2}k_1 = \frac{h}{2}f$, so

    \[ k_2 = f + \frac{h}{2}\,ff' + \frac{h^2}{8}\,f^2f'' + \frac{h^3}{48}\,f^3f''' + \Oh{h^4}. \]

  • $k_3$: here $\delta = \frac{h}{2}k_2 = \frac{h}{2}f + \frac{h^2}{4}ff' + \frac{h^3}{16}f^2f'' + \cdots$, with $\delta^2 = \frac{h^2}{4}f^2 + \frac{h^3}{4}f^2f' + \cdots$ and $\delta^3 = \frac{h^3}{8}f^3 + \cdots$. Then

    \[ \begin{aligned} \delta f' &= \frac{h}{2}\,ff' + \frac{h^2}{4}\,ff'^2 + \frac{h^3}{16}\,f^2f'f'' + \cdots, \\ \frac{\delta^2}{2}\,f'' &= \frac{h^2}{8}\,f^2f'' + \frac{h^3}{8}\,f^2f'f'' + \cdots, \\ \frac{\delta^3}{6}\,f''' &= \frac{h^3}{48}\,f^3f''' + \cdots, \end{aligned} \]

    and adding up $\bigl(\frac1{16} + \frac18 = \frac3{16}\bigr)$,

    \[ k_3 = f + \frac{h}{2}\,ff' + h^2\Bigl(\frac14\,ff'^2 + \frac18\,f^2f''\Bigr) + h^3\Bigl(\frac3{16}\,f^2f'f'' + \frac1{48}\,f^3f'''\Bigr) + \Oh{h^4}. \]

  • $k_4$: here $\delta = hk_3 = hf + \frac{h^2}{2}ff' + h^3\bigl(\frac14ff'^2 + \frac18f^2f''\bigr) + \cdots$, with $\delta^2 = h^2f^2 + h^3f^2f' + \cdots$ and $\delta^3 = h^3f^3 + \cdots$. Then

    \[ \begin{aligned} \delta f' &= h\,ff' + \frac{h^2}{2}\,ff'^2 + h^3\Bigl(\frac14\,ff'^3 + \frac18\,f^2f'f''\Bigr) + \cdots, \\ \frac{\delta^2}{2}\,f'' &= \frac{h^2}{2}\,f^2f'' + \frac{h^3}{2}\,f^2f'f'' + \cdots, \\ \frac{\delta^3}{6}\,f''' &= \frac{h^3}{6}\,f^3f''' + \cdots, \end{aligned} \]

    and adding up $\bigl(\frac18 + \frac12 = \frac58\bigr)$,

    \[ k_4 = f + h\,ff' + h^2\Bigl(\frac12\,ff'^2 + \frac12\,f^2f''\Bigr) + h^3\Bigl(\frac14\,ff'^3 + \frac58\,f^2f'f'' + \frac16\,f^3f'''\Bigr) + \Oh{h^4}. \]

The weighted sum. Collect the coefficients of $k_1 + 2k_2 + 2k_3 + k_4$, term by term:

$f$$h\,ff'$ $h^2ff'^2$$h^2f^2f''$ $h^3ff'^3$$h^3f^2f'f''$ $h^3f^3f'''$
$k_1$$1$$0$$0$$0$$0$$0$$0$
$2k_2$$2$$1$$0$$\frac14$$0$$0$$\frac1{24}$
$2k_3$$2$$1$$\frac12$$\frac14$$0$$\frac38$$\frac1{24}$
$k_4$$1$$1$$\frac12$$\frac12$$\frac14$$\frac58$$\frac16$
sum$6$$3$$1$$1$$\frac14$$1$$\frac14$

So

\[ k_1 + 2k_2 + 2k_3 + k_4 = 6f + 3h\,ff' + h^2(ff'^2 + f^2f'') + \frac{h^3}{4}\,(ff'^3 + 4f^2f'f'' + f^3f''') + \Oh{h^4}, \]

and multiplying by $h/6$,

\[ Y_{n+1} = Y_n + hf + \frac{h^2}{2}\,ff' + \frac{h^3}{6}\,(ff'^2 + f^2f'') + \frac{h^4}{24}\,(ff'^3 + 4f^2f'f'' + f^3f''') + \Oh{h^5}. \]

This is the Taylor series of the true solution, term by term up to $h^4$. One step makes an error of order $h^5$, and over a fixed time the error is of order $h^4$: RK4 is fourth order. The half steps and the weights $1, 2, 2, 1$ are what make every column of the table agree with the Taylor series (Kutta found them in 1901). The third stage is needed for this: with $4k_2$ in place of $2k_2 + 2k_3$, the $h^2ff'^2$ column would sum to $\frac12$ instead of 1. For a system the same calculation has more terms, since $f'$ becomes the Jacobian matrix and $f''$ a bilinear form, and RK4 matches them as well.

Example S.1 (One RK4 step for $\dot y = y$). Given. $\dot y = y$, $y(0) = 1$, one step $h = 0.5$.

Find. What are $k_1, \ldots, k_4$ and $Y_1$? How does $Y_1$ compare with $e^{0.5}$, and with Simpson's rule for the same step (Example 2.1)?

Answer. Each slope is the trial value itself, since $f(y) = y$:

\[ \begin{aligned} &k_1 = 1, && \\ &Y_0 + 0.25\,k_1 = 1.25, && k_2 = 1.25, \\ &Y_0 + 0.25\,k_2 = 1.3125, && k_3 = 1.3125, \\ &Y_0 + 0.5\,k_3 = 1.65625, && k_4 = 1.65625. \end{aligned} \]

Then $k_1 + 2k_2 + 2k_3 + k_4 = 1 + 2.5 + 2.625 + 1.65625 = 7.78125$ and

\[ Y_1 = 1 + \frac{0.5}{6}\cdot 7.78125 = 1.6484375, \]

which is also $R(0.5) = 1 + 0.5 + 0.125 + 0.0208333 + 0.0026042$. The exact value is $e^{0.5} = 1.6487213$, so the error is $0.00028$, close to the first missing term $\frac{z^5}{120} = 0.00026$. Simpson's rule with the true values $1$, $e^{0.25}$ and $e^{0.5}$ gives $1.6487352$, an error of $-0.000014$. RK4 has to predict those values: its midpoint slope is $\frac12(k_2 + k_3) = 1.28125$ against $e^{0.25} = 1.28403$, and its end slope $1.65625$ against $1.64872$. Most of its error comes from these predictions. The sketch shows the step as an area.

Runge–Kutta in practice

Library solvers such as MATLAB's ode45 and SciPy's solve_ivp (by default) use a pair of Runge–Kutta formulas of orders 4 and 5, due to Dormand and Prince, that share their evaluations of $\vec f$. The difference between the two results estimates the error of the step, and the solver adjusts $h$ to keep that estimate below a tolerance.
