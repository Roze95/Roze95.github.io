Resources

The typed lecture notes and Strang's book are sufficient to do well in this subject. Everything below is for when you want a second voice on a topic, a proof the lecture skipped, or a harder problem to chew on.

The text and its companion site

Computational Science and Engineering — G. Strang, Wellesley–Cambridge Press. The course text. The companion site carries the full table of contents, two free sample sections (§1.1 Four Special Matrices — read it before Lecture 2 — and §4.1 Fourier Series for Periodic Functions), and dozens of MATLAB codes organised by chapter. Start here before anything else on this page.
Gilbert Strang's page — MIT Mathematics. Links to his other texts, including the finite-element supplement that is useful around Lectures 15–17, and to every one of his video series.

Video lectures

18.085, Fall 2008 — Strang, MIT OpenCourseWare. The whole subject on video, taught by the author of the book. Ordering and emphasis differ from ours in places, so use it topic by topic rather than as a parallel course. Assignments and exams come with solutions.
18.06 Linear Algebra — Strang, MIT OpenCourseWare. If the first three weeks feel fast, this is the prerequisite done properly. Lectures on column space, elimination, projection and eigenvalues map almost one-to-one onto our Lectures 1–8.
Essence of Linear Algebra — 3Blue1Brown. Fifteen short animated episodes. Watch chapters 3–4 (linear transformations, composition) alongside Lecture 1 — they are the geometric picture behind “matrix multiplication is composition”, and they are the fastest way to stop seeing a matrix as a grid of numbers.

Arc 1 — Linear algebra and discretisation Lectures 1–11

Numerical Linear Algebra — Trefethen & Bau, SIAM. Forty short lectures, beautifully written. Lectures 7–10 are the cleanest account of QR and Gram–Schmidt you will find, and Lectures 11–12 do least squares. Read these around our Lectures 10–11.
Finite Difference Methods for Ordinary and Partial Differential Equations — R. J. LeVeque, SIAM. The reference for everything we do with difference stencils, boundary conditions and truncation error, with far more care about convergence and stability than we have time for. The author posts exercises with MATLAB code, chapter by chapter.
18.335 Introduction to Numerical Methods — MIT OpenCourseWare, and the live course repository. Where 18.085 goes next if you like the linear-algebra half: conditioning, floating point, QR and eigenvalue algorithms, Krylov methods. The repo's lecture notes are current and free.

Arc 2 — Equilibrium and finite elements Lectures 12–17

Strang, chapters 2–3 — plus the MATLAB codes on the same page. The ATCA framework, springs and masses, trusses, and 1D finite elements are the core of these chapters, and the posted codes let you assemble the stiffness matrix yourself rather than reading about it.
18.086 Mathematical Methods for Engineers II — Strang, MIT OpenCourseWare. The sequel: initial-value problems, iterative solvers, network flows, optimisation. Useful now for its treatment of time-stepping, which we only touch in Lecture 14.

Arc 3 — Fourier methods Lectures 18–24

Strang, chapter 4 — §4.1 is free on the companion site. Fourier series for periodic functions, the DFT, convolution. Read §4.1 the weekend before Lecture 19.
18.335 notes on the FFT — S. G. Johnson (author of FFTW) and successors. If you want to know why the FFT is $O(n\log n)$ and what a real implementation worries about — cache, radix choice, numerical stability — this is the short, authoritative account. Pairs with Lecture 21.
Spectral Methods in MATLAB — L. N. Trefethen, SIAM. Forty short programs showing what happens when you replace finite differences by global trigonometric or polynomial interpolation. The natural sequel to our Fourier arc, and every program is a page long.

Computing

Scientific Python Lectures — the NumPy / SciPy / Matplotlib ecosystem, from scratch. If you are taking the Python track and have not used numpy seriously, work through “Getting started” in the first week. The sparse-array chapter matters from Lecture 2 onward — our matrices are banded and you should not be storing the zeros.
Chebfun — open-source MATLAB package, no toolboxes required. Computes with functions instead of samples, to about 15 digits. Not needed for any assignment, but it is the most direct way to see what discretisation costs you: solve the same boundary-value problem with our difference matrix and with Chebfun, and compare.
Sanity habits Check every difference scheme by halving $h$ and watching the error ratio — 2 for first order, 4 for second. Check every matrix you assemble on the smallest case you can do by hand ($n = 3$ or $4$). Most lost points on this subject's computational problems come from skipping one of these two.

Practice under exam conditions

The OCW 18.085 page above carries a decade of Strang's problem sets and exams with solutions. Use them the way you would use a past paper: work the problem cold, on paper, with one sheet of notes and a clock, and only then open the solution. Reading solutions is not practice.

A caution: those solutions are public and widely circulated. This term's sets keep the problem architecture you would recognise but change every number, so a memorised solution is worth nothing on Gradescope.
