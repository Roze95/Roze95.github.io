Quantum Computing — Library Notes

Scripts 01–07 · Single-qubit foundations · Built with Qiskit 2.4.1 + qiskit-aer 0.17.2

This page summarizes the first five scripts of the library. Every script is a single qubit. The goal across the set is to internalize three things: gates are deterministic unitary matrices, measurement is a separate probabilistic projection, and the choice of measurement basis determines what you can see.

Core machinery (used everywhere)

A qubit is a 2-dimensional complex vector

$|0\rangle = \binom{1}{0}, \quad |1\rangle = \binom{0}{1}$. A general state is $\alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$ with $|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1$.

A gate is a 2×2 unitary matrix

It acts on the state vector by ordinary matrix multiplication. No randomness. Every shot, the same operation.

Measurement is a separate, non-unitary step

Z-basis measurement returns 0 with probability $|\alpha|^2$ and 1 with probability $|\beta|^2$ (Born rule). All randomness in these scripts comes from this step alone.

Script 01 — Measure $|0\rangle$

qc = QuantumCircuit(1, 1)
qc.measure(0, 0)
# Result: {'0': 1024}

Circuit

     ┌─┐
  q: ┤M├
     └╥┘
c: 1/═╩═
      0

One quantum wire q, one classical wire c. The double line is the classical bus; the M box collapses q into bit 0 of c.

No gate applied. Qubits start in $|0\rangle$, measurement returns 0 deterministically.

Takeaway: Default state is $|0\rangle$. Born rule on $|0\rangle$: $P(0)=1$.

Script 02 — X gate

qc.x(0)
qc.measure(0, 0)
# Result: {'1': 1024}

Circuit

     ┌───┐┌─┐
  q: ┤ X ├┤M├
     └───┘└╥┘
c: 1/══════╩═
           0

$X$ flips $|0\rangle \to |1\rangle$ before measurement.

$X$ is the bit-flip / NOT gate:

$$X = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad X|0\rangle = |1\rangle$$

Takeaway: $X$ is deterministic. Output is 1 on every shot.

Script 03 — Hadamard

qc.h(0)
qc.measure(0, 0)
# Result: {'0': ~512, '1': ~512}

Circuit

     ┌───┐┌─┐
  q: ┤ H ├┤M├
     └───┘└╥┘
c: 1/══════╩═
           0

$H$ rotates $|0\rangle$ into $|+\rangle$; the Z-basis measurement then collapses to 0 or 1 with equal probability.

$$H = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad H|0\rangle = |+\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt 2}(|0\rangle + |1\rangle)$$

Two important properties of $H$:

  • Self-inverse: $H^2 = I$. Apply $H$ twice and you get $|0\rangle$ back exactly.
  • Change of basis: $H$'s columns are $|+\rangle$ and $|-\rangle$ — it is literally the change-of-basis matrix between the Z basis and the X basis.
Takeaway: The gate is still deterministic — every qubit ends up in the same state $|+\rangle$. The 50/50 outcome is purely a property of the Z-basis measurement.

Script 04 — Ry(θ): tunable Born rule

theta = math.pi / 3
qc.ry(theta, 0)
qc.measure(0, 0)
# Predicted P(0)=0.75, P(1)=0.25 → expect ~768/256
# Observed:  {'0': 788, '1': 236}

Circuit

     ┌─────────┐┌─┐
  q: ┤ Ry(π/3) ├┤M├
     └─────────┘└╥┘
c: 1/════════════╩═
                 0

$R_y(\pi/3)$ rotates $|0\rangle$ by 60° around the y-axis. The angle is shown inside the gate box.

$$R_y(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos(\theta/2) & -\sin(\theta/2) \\ \sin(\theta/2) & \cos(\theta/2) \end{pmatrix}$$

Acting on $|0\rangle$:

$$R_y(\theta)|0\rangle = \cos(\theta/2)|0\rangle + \sin(\theta/2)|1\rangle, \quad P(0) = \cos^2(\theta/2)$$

θEffect on $|0\rangle$Equivalent gate (Z-basis stats)
0$|0\rangle$identity (script 01)
π/3$P(0)=3/4$—
π/2$|+\rangle$like H (script 03)
π$|1\rangle$like X (script 02)
The half-angle isn't a typo. Qubits live in SU(2), which double-covers ordinary 3D rotations (SO(3)). A $2\pi$ rotation introduces a $-1$ phase; you need $4\pi$ to truly return. This factor of 1/2 is geometric, not a convention.
$H \ne R_y(\pi/2)$ as matrices, even though they produce the same result on $|0\rangle$. Strict identity: $H = R_y(\pi/2)\cdot Z$. They diverge when applied to $|1\rangle$.
Takeaway: One slider $\theta$ unifies the trivial case, X, and H. The Born rule turns angles into probabilities via $\cos^2$ and $\sin^2$.

Script 05 — Z gate: the "invisible" phase

Z = [[1,  0],
     [0, -1]]

Circuit (canonical case: $Z|+\rangle = |-\rangle$, measured in Z)

     ┌───┐┌───┐┌─┐
  q: ┤ H ├┤ Z ├┤M├
     └───┘└───┘└╥┘
c: 1/═══════════╩═
                0

First $H$ prepares $|+\rangle$; then $Z$ flips it to $|-\rangle$. The Z-basis measurement reports ~50/50 — identical to running just $H$ alone (script 03). The state changed; the measurement can't see it.

$Z|0\rangle = |0\rangle$, $Z|1\rangle = -|1\rangle$, $Z|+\rangle = |-\rangle$.

The script ran four configurations and printed both the statevector (deterministic) and the Z-basis measurement counts:

StateStatevectorZ-basis counts
$|0\rangle$(1, 0)all 0
$Z|0\rangle$(1, 0)all 0
$|+\rangle$(0.707, +0.707)~50/50
$|-\rangle = Z|+\rangle$(0.707, −0.707)~50/50

The bottom two rows are different states — the sign on the second amplitude is real — but the Z-basis measurement cannot tell them apart. The Born rule on Z-basis measurement only sees $|\text{amplitude}|^2$, and squaring kills the sign.

The deep idea. A measurement is a projection onto a chosen basis. Z-basis measurement asks "$|0\rangle$ or $|1\rangle$?" — it cannot ask "$|+\rangle$ or $|-\rangle$?". To detect the phase, you must change the question by changing the basis.
Takeaway: Phase information is real but basis-dependent in what it reveals. This is the foundation of QKD: Alice and Bob's basis choices, not their bit values, are what carry the security guarantee.

Script 06 — Measuring in the X basis

qc.h(0)             # the basis-change trick
qc.measure(0, 0)

Circuit (a): measure $|0\rangle$ in the X basis

     ┌───┐┌─┐
  q: ┤ H ├┤M├
     └───┘└╥┘
c: 1/══════╩═
           0

No state preparation — qubit is just $|0\rangle$. The lone $H$ is the X-basis measurement recipe. Result: 50/50, because $|0\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt 2}(|+\rangle + |-\rangle)$ has equal weight on both X-basis outcomes.

Circuit (b): prepare $|+\rangle$, then measure in the X basis

     ┌───┐ ░ ┌───┐┌─┐
  q: ┤ H ├─░─┤ H ├┤M├
     └───┘ ░ └───┘└╥┘
c: 1/══════════════╩═
                   0

Two $H$'s with a barrier (the dotted line) marking the boundary between state preparation and measurement basis change. Algebraically $H \cdot H = I$, so the qubit reaches the measurement still in $|0\rangle$, giving deterministic 0. Conceptually: we prepared $|+\rangle$ and asked an X-basis question — perfect alignment, no randomness.

Hardware can only measure in the Z basis. To measure in the X basis, apply $H$ before measure. Since $H$ is its own inverse and swaps $|+\rangle \leftrightarrow |0\rangle$ and $|-\rangle \leftrightarrow |1\rangle$, this rotates X-basis questions into Z-basis answers.

Compare to script 05: the same four states, now measured in X instead of Z. The roles flip completely.

StateZ-basis counts (script 05)X-basis counts (script 06)
$|0\rangle$all 0~50/50
$|1\rangle$all 1~50/50
$|+\rangle$~50/50all 0
$|-\rangle$~50/50all 1

The phase that was invisible in script 05 is now the only thing that matters.

The deep idea, finalized. A quantum measurement is a question with a built-in vocabulary. The basis you choose decides which states get crisp answers and which get coin flips. There is no "true" answer in the absence of a basis choice — the basis is part of the experiment.

Why this is the engine of QKD

You now have everything for BB84 at a mechanical level:

  1. Alice randomly picks a bit and a basis. She prepares one of four states: $|0\rangle$, $|1\rangle$, $|+\rangle$, $|-\rangle$.
  2. Bob randomly picks a basis (Z or X) to measure in.
  3. When their bases match (~half the time), Bob's bit equals Alice's bit deterministically — the situation in scripts 01/02/06.
  4. When their bases mismatch, Bob gets 50/50 — the situation in scripts 03/05.
  5. They publicly compare bases (not bits!) and discard mismatched rounds. Matched rounds form a shared secret key.
  6. An eavesdropper Eve doesn't know Alice's basis, so she has to guess. Wrong-basis measurements disturb the state, which Alice and Bob detect as errors during a public sanity-check round.

Script 07 — Phase gates S, T and the third Pauli basis

The phase-gate hierarchy is a sequence of halvings, all of them rotations around the Bloch z-axis:

$$Z = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\;\text{(π)} \qquad S = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & i \end{pmatrix}\;\text{(π/2)} \qquad T = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & e^{i\pi/4} \end{pmatrix}\;\text{(π/4)}$$

Algebraic relationships: $Z = S^2 = T^4$ and $S = T^2$. All three leave $|\text{amplitude}|^2$ unchanged, so all three are invisible in the Z basis.

But $S$ is also invisible in the X basis. $S|+\rangle$ produces a complex relative phase, and $H$ alone can't reveal that. We need a third measurement direction — the Y basis.

BasisReadsRecipe before measure
Z$|0\rangle$ vs $|1\rangle$(nothing)
X$|+\rangle$ vs $|-\rangle$apply $H$
Y$|+i\rangle$ vs $|-i\rangle$apply $S^\dagger$, then $H$

The Y-basis recipe works because $H S^\dagger |+i\rangle = |0\rangle$ — the Y-basis question gets rotated into a Z-basis answer, exactly like the X-basis trick from script 06.

Circuit (a): $S$ gate — prepare $|+i\rangle$, measure in the Y basis

     ┌───┐┌───┐ ░ ┌─────┐┌───┐┌─┐
  q: ┤ H ├┤ S ├─░─┤ Sdg ├┤ H ├┤M├
     └───┘└───┘ ░ └─────┘└───┘└╥┘
c: 1/══════════════════════════╩═
                               0

Left of the barrier: state prep — $H$ then $S$ produces $S|+\rangle = |+i\rangle$. Right of the barrier: Y-basis measurement recipe ($S^\dagger$, then $H$, then measure). Result: deterministic 0. The same state measured in Z or X would give 50/50.

Circuit (b): $T$ gate — prepare $T|+\rangle$, measure in the X basis

     ┌───┐┌───┐ ░ ┌───┐┌─┐
  q: ┤ H ├┤ T ├─░─┤ H ├┤M├
     └───┘└───┘ ░ └───┘└╥┘
c: 1/═══════════════════╩═
                        0

$T|+\rangle$ sits at 45° between the +X and +Y axes on the Bloch equator. X-basis measurement gives ~85% 0 — biased but not deterministic, because the state is half-aligned with the X axis.

The full result table

StateZ basisX basisY basis
$|+\rangle$50/50100% → 050/50
$S|+\rangle = |+i\rangle$50/5050/50100% → 0

Each Pauli basis "owns" one Bloch axis. To detect a rotation around an axis, measure along that same axis. Look the wrong way and you get the maximally uninformative 50/50.

The T-gate result is the prettiest math. $T|+\rangle$ sits at 45° between the +X and +Y axes on the Bloch equator, so X and Y both give the same biased answer: $$P(0 \mid \text{X}) = P(0 \mid \text{Y}) = \cos^2(\pi/8) = \frac{2 + \sqrt 2}{4} \approx 0.854$$ Sim measured 86% and 85% — within shot noise of the prediction. Continuous Born rule, just along a different axis.
Why $T$ matters: universality. The set $\{H, T\}$ — just two gates — can approximate any single-qubit unitary to arbitrary precision (Solovay–Kitaev). $H$ gives you basis-change capability, $T$ gives you fine-grained z-axis rotation. Combined, they reach any point on the Bloch sphere. Adding $T$ to the Clifford group $\{H, S, \text{CNOT}\}$ promotes it from "classically simulable" to "universal for quantum computation."
Takeaway: The Bloch sphere has three orthogonal measurement axes (X, Y, Z), and each gate's effect is most visible in one specific basis. Continuous gates (like $T$) sit between two axes and give partial information in both. This three-axis picture is the geometric backbone of all single-qubit quantum mechanics.

BB84 — The four states

BB84 (and Sara's HD-QKD work, in spirit) is built from exactly four states. Two bases × two bit values = four. In the QKD literature these bases are usually called + (rectilinear) and × (diagonal), which match the Z basis and X basis from the scripts:

PolarizationQubit stateBasisBitRecipe from $\|0\rangle$
0° (horizontal)$|0\rangle$+ (Z)0(nothing)
90° (vertical)$|1\rangle$+ (Z)1X
45° (diagonal)$|+\rangle$× (X)0H
135° (anti-diagonal, or −45°)$|-\rangle$× (X)1H, then Z

The four prep circuits

                         ┌───┐
  q: ─────         q: ────┤ X ├────
                          └───┘
       |0⟩, 0°              |1⟩, 90°

     ┌───┐              ┌───┐┌───┐
  q: ┤ H ├          q: ─┤ H ├┤ Z ├─
     └───┘              └───┘└───┘
       |+⟩, 45°            |-⟩, 135°

All four BB84 states. Same starting point ($|0\rangle$), four different gate sequences, four different polarization angles.

Geometric picture

Place the four states on a clock face. The two bases sit at right angles to each other in real polarization space:

           90°  (|1⟩)
            │
   135°─────┼─────45°
   (|-⟩)    │     (|+⟩)
            │
            0°  (|0⟩)

The Pauli gates have clean geometric meanings:

  • X flips top–bottom: 0° ↔ 90°   ($|0\rangle \leftrightarrow |1\rangle$). Bit-flip within the + basis.
  • Z flips left–right: 45° ↔ 135°   ($|+\rangle \leftrightarrow |-\rangle$). Bit-flip within the × basis.
  • H swaps the two axes: 0° ↔ 45°, 90° ↔ 135°. The basis-change gate.
The polarization "factor of 2". $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ are 180° apart on the Bloch sphere, but only 90° apart in real polarization. Same with $|+\rangle$ vs $|-\rangle$. This is the same factor of 2 you saw in $R_y(\theta)$ — the SU(2) double cover of SO(3). Polarization rotations live in real 3D space (SO(3)); qubit states live in its double cover (SU(2)).
Memory hook for the bit-flip rules. Each Pauli is the bit-flip of its own basis:
  • X is the bit-flip of the Z basis ($|0\rangle \leftrightarrow |1\rangle$) — used to make the bit-1 state in the + basis.
  • Z is the bit-flip of the X basis ($|+\rangle \leftrightarrow |-\rangle$) — used after $H$ to make the bit-1 state in the × basis.
So the symmetric recipe is: pick your basis (no gate or $H$), then optionally apply that basis's bit-flip ($X$ or $Z$).
Takeaway: BB84 is a 1-qubit protocol that recycles four prep recipes you already know — identity, X, H, HZ — applied with random bit and basis choices each round. Everything else (sifting, error checking, key extraction) is classical post-processing.

Cross-cutting principles

  1. Gates are deterministic. The 2×2 unitary maps every input state to one specific output state. No randomness.
  2. Measurements are probabilistic. All "quantum randomness" comes from the measurement step, not the gate.
  3. The basis is a choice. The same physical state gives different statistics depending on which basis you measure in. $H$ swaps Z↔X; $S^\dagger H$ swaps Z↔Y.
  4. Phase is invisible until you switch bases. $|+\rangle$ vs $|-\rangle$ look identical in Z but trivially distinguishable in X. $|+i\rangle$ vs $|-i\rangle$ look identical in both Z and X — you need Y.
  5. The Bloch sphere is real. Three orthogonal axes (X, Y, Z), three measurement bases, and any single-qubit state is one point on the surface.
